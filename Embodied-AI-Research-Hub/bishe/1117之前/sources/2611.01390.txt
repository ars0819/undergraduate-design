Same Path, Less Time: Training-Free Fast Execution of
Vision-Language-Action Models under Inference Latency
Fuyi Chen
University of Nottingham Ningbo China
Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) models predict chunks of future actions, yet deployed robots waste time in
two ways that are independent of model capability: they halt while the next chunk is inferred, and they
move at the speed of slow, cautious demonstrations. Existing remedies remove one loss each, and stacking
them fails, because asynchrony and speed-up each change the correspondence between planned progress
and executed motion. We presentPath-preserving Time Scaling(PTS), a training-free execution layer for
frozen action-chunking policies built on one principle:the robot’s motion is a time reparameterization
of a reference path, and the path changes only in response to new observations.A path layer decodes
every replan deterministically in one step and enters it at the progress consumed during inference. A time
layer sums the planned increments, keeps the arm at 1×while the gripper moves, and caps the rate by the
actuator’slinearrangeandbychunkfeasibilityunderlatency. Weshowthatmisalignedentryandstochastic
sampling add a bias and a variance to the continuation error, and derive a break-even success rate that
states when speeding up pays off. On LIBERO with two frozen VLAs under injected inference latency,
PTS raises task throughput over the strongest deployable baseline by 33% for𝜋0.5 and 104% for SmolVLA
while matching or improving the success rate, is best on all six model–suite combinations, and exceeds
zero-latency 1× execution. Against asynchronous 1× execution with the same one-step decoder, the time
layer alone adds 28% and 38%. On LIBERO-Dynamic it improves on stop-and-wait execution by 38% and
is comparable to asynchronous 1×execution.
1 Introduction
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024; Physical Intelligence
et al., 2025; Shukor et al., 2025) predictaction chunks, short sequences of future actions (Zhao et al., 2023; Chi et al.,
2023). The default deployment is synchronous: infer a chunk, execute part of it, stop, and infer again. This wastes time
in two ways that do not depend on how capable the model is. First, the robot stands still during every inference: if one
𝜋0.5 inference takes about 150ms, a 20Hz robot pauses for three steps after every eight it executes. Second, execution is
locked to the speed of the demonstrations, which are typically slow, cautious teleoperation. Figure 1a shows both losses
in one episode. This paper removes both for afrozenpolicy, without any training.
Why the two fixes do not compose.Each loss has a known remedy. Asynchronous execution, e.g., real-time chunking
(RTC) (Black et al., 2025a), keeps the robot moving during inference but executes at 1×. Training-free speed-up such
as FreeSpeed (Hu et al., 2026) amplifies the actions but assumes synchronous execution. Stacking the two fails: run
asynchronously, FreeSpeed drops the success rate of𝜋0.5 on our LIBERO-Spatial development set from 45/50 to 20/50,
even with the same gripper handling as our method; keeping its speed profile but summing the planned increments
instead of resampling them restores 47/50. The cause is bookkeeping. A chunk is a plan that starts at the moment its
image was taken, and by the time it arrives the robot has already consumed part of it; speed-up changes how much. If
the executor loses track of how much of the plan has actually been executed, the robot repeats or skips motion and
leaves the planned path (Figure 2c).
Key idea: separate the path from its timing.An action chunk defines a planned path. If execution changes only the
timingalong that path, i.e., the robot follows the path the model planned but traverses it faster and never stops during
inference, then speeding up reduces to shortening time inside a feasible set. This separation of path and time underlies
time-optimal path parameterization (TOPP) in robotics (Bobrow et al., 1985; Shin and McKay, 1985; Pham and Pham,
2018). Learned policies add two ingredients that classical TOPP lacks: closed-loopreplanningmakes the path a
concatenationofchunks,andinference latencymeanstherobothasmovedonbythetimeanewchunkarrives. Execution

2025); forrobotpolicies,consistencydistillation(Prasadetal.,2024)andMean-Flow(Chenetal.,2026a)yieldone-step
VLAs. Pan et al. (2025) argue that generative control does not owe its advantage mainly to multimodality. Chen et al.
(2026b) show that one Euler step from the noise end returns the conditional mean for an ideal velocity field and evaluate

our contribution is its coordination with asynchronous execution and time scaling.
Path parameterization and receding-horizon control.TOPP computes the minimum-time traversal of a fixed path
under dynamic constraints (Bobrow et al., 1985; Shin and McKay, 1985), by convex optimization (Verscheure et al.,
2009), numerical integration (Kunz and Stilman, 2012), or reachability analysis (Pham and Pham, 2018); under
pointwise decoupled bounds the optimum saturates the bound. PTS’s time layer is this case applied to chunk execution
(Section A.3); what is new is where the bounds come from: the actuator’s linear range, chunk feasibility under latency,
and a rate cap selected from data. Receding-horizon control executes the beginning of each newly solved plan (Mayne
et al., 2000); the path layer extends this practice to delayed inference and accelerated consumption of the plan.

changes only in response to new observations.The second clause yields the path layer (Section 4.1), the first the time
layer (Section 4.2); Section 4.3 selects the rate cap. Algorithm 1 gives the complete procedure, and Section A the
derivations and assumptions.

4.3 Choosing the rate cap: when does speed-up pay off?

5.1 Setup
Benchmark and policies.We use the four LIBERO suites (Liu et al., 2023a) (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long; 10 tasks each)

targets. We evaluate two frozen LIBERO-finetuned checkpoints released through LeRobot (Cadene et al., 2026):𝜋0.5
(Physical Intelligence et al., 2025) and SmolVLA (Shukor et al., 2025), both with𝐻=50 . SmolVLA is evaluated on
Object and Goal; on Spatial its 1×success rate is about 0.5 and results are dominated by episode noise.
Latency.Latency is injected in control steps, converted from measured inference times:𝜋0.5 𝑑10 =3 and 𝑑1 =2 ;
SmolVLA 𝑑10 =5 and 𝑑1 =1 (Section B.2). RTC’s guidance overhead is charged separately (1.79×, 𝑑=6 ). During
these 𝑑 steps the simulator advances with the executing method’s commands (stop-and-wait methods hold position), and
time is counted in 50ms control steps; Section 5.4 varies𝑑.
Protocol.The development set is used to select¯𝑟(𝜋0.5: Spatial initial states 0–14; SmolVLA: Object and Goal, 0–4).
The test set consists of initial states 20–29 of every task, i.e., 100 episodes per suite and method, with identical seeds
across methods. The primary metric isΘ (Equation (1)); 95% intervals come from a task-stratified paired bootstrap
(Efron, 1979) with 20,000 resamples. All methods use𝑠=8 ; ¯𝑟=1.5 for 𝜋0.5 and 2for SmolVLA, selected with PTS-𝐾
and reused without reselection.
Baselines.Sync-1 × (stop and wait);Async-1× (keep executing during inference; switch to the new sampled chunk

6 Limitations
Assumptions and scope of the analysis.The time-layer rules preservecommands; how far this carries over to poses
depends on actuator linearity, which we identified in simulation (Section A.1). Proposition 1 constrains only the
remaining net displacement and relies on conditional independence and prefix consistency. That one-step decoding does
not lower success is an empirical finding on two models, and Proposition 2 is an accounting identity that interprets
rather than predicts the choice of¯𝑟.
Deterministic decoding.One-step decoding is close to the conditional mean only for an accurate field, and the mean can
fall between modes when a task requires choosing between clearly different actions (Section A.2).𝐾-step sampling with
fixed noise keeps the path deterministic in that case, at the cost of latency. The measured one-step output differs from
the mean of 10-step samples, which suggests that its benefit comes from being deterministic and fast rather than from
averaging.
Rate cap and actuator. ¯𝑟is selected on a development set and reflects the joint precision tolerance of policy, actuator,
latency, and task distribution; the one-step version reuses the cap selected with PTS-𝐾. On the Panda𝑅body ≥2≥¯𝑟 , so
the rate is uniform outside gripper windows, and with a weakened actuator the adaptive rate did not beat a uniform one.
Evaluation.All results are in simulation with injected latency; there are no real-robot experiments, where reset and
timeout costs would change𝑃∗ but not the form of Proposition 2. The static results use one set of test initial states, and
the dynamic results 30 episodes per suite, which limits statistical power. With moving targets, PTS does not improve on
asynchronous 1×execution; selecting or adapting¯𝑟for dynamic tasks is left open.
7 Conclusion
PTS brings the separation of path and time from classical trajectory planning to the execution of learned action chunks.
The path layer lets the reference path change only with new observations, through deterministic one-step decoding and
entry at the consumed progress; the time layer changes only the timing along that path, through incremental summation,

gripper synchronization, and a rate limit. Without training, PTS raises the task throughput of frozen𝜋0.5 by 33% and of
SmolVLA by 104% over the strongest deployable baseline (28% and 38% over the same one-step decoder at 1×), and
exceeds zero-latency 1× execution on both. The same principle applies to any policy that outputs relative-increment
chunks; testing it on real robots and with contact-aware rate caps remains future work.

exactly one denoising step is made, and that post-processing matches the original pipeline. The command jerk in
Figure 5c is, per episode, the mean absolute second difference of the executed 6-D arm commands over moving steps.
B.5 Baselines
All methods share the policy wrapper, the latency model, the replanning interval𝑠=8 , the environment seeds, and the
episode horizons of LIBERO.
• Zero-latency 1×: 10-step sampling,𝑑=0 . A reference for what the policy achieves without any inference delay; not
deployable.
•Sync-1×: execute𝑠plan steps, then hold for𝑑steps while the next chunk is inferred.
• Async-1×: request a new chunk every𝑠 consumed steps and keep executing; on arrival, switch to the new sampled
chunk at the consumed progress𝑚(the standard practice of skipping the stale prefix).
• RTC(Black et al., 2025a): Async-1× with guided inpainting following the paper: exponentially decaying soft mask,
maximum guidance weight𝛽=5 , guidance over the entire remaining part of the previous chunk, and the frozen
prefix equal to the consumed delay. The guidance overhead is charged as latency (𝑑=6).
• FreeSpeed(Hu et al., 2026): synchronous, commanded rate𝜌=2 . Each step resamples the plan at rate𝜌 and scales
thetranslationandrotationincrementsby 𝑓=1+ (0.9𝜌−1)exp(−50𝐼 cos),where 𝐼cos isthedirectionalinconsistency
of adjacent translation increments. Following the paper,𝑓 is computed once per cycle over the plan steps executed in
that cycle. The gripper command is passed through unscaled.
•Sync-2×: synchronous, uniform rate 2 with incremental summation (R2), no gripper synchronization.
• PTS-𝐾: PTS with10-stepstochasticsampling instead ofone-stepdecoding, and 𝑑10 insteadof 𝑑1 inlatency and 𝑅feas.
•One-step-1×: Async-1×with deterministic one-step decoding and𝑑1.
B.6 Dynamic scene
LIBERO-Dynamic (Ren et al., 2026) runs in a separate server process and latency is injected in control steps as in the
static experiments; the target keeps moving during the injected inference steps, including those in which stop-and-wait
methods hold position. We use target speed 0.06m/s, trajectory 0 of each task at the three motion levels provided by the
benchmark, and 10 tasks per suite. Latency settings are as in the static experiments.
