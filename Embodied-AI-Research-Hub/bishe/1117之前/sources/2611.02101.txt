When Delay Meets Drift: Physical Safety of Robot
Foundation Models Is a Cross-Layer Problem
Liangkai Liu, Bimal Thapa Magar
Texas Tech University
{liangkai.liu, bthapama}@ttu.edu
Abstract:Robot foundation models now act in a closed loop, yet their safety is
evaluated one variation at a time: robustness benchmarks perturb the scene while
assuming instantaneous inference, and real-time execution methods study infer-
ence delay in nominal scenes. We argue that physical safety is not a property of
the model alone, but emerges from how variations in the physical environment, the
model, the computing platform, and execution interact in closed loop. We formal-
ize this ascross-layer variation propagationacross these system layers and use
an interaction contrast to measure when joint variation creates more risk than the
sum of its parts. In 4,000 paired closed-loop episodes of theπ0.5 vision-language-
action model on LIBERO, a 400 ms inference delay raises the safety-violation rate
by 3.5 points under the training viewpoint but by 37 points under a60 ◦ camera
shift: 33.5 points (95% CI 25–43) more than the two effects combined. We close
with a research agenda towardpredictable physical intelligence: predicting when
variation becomes risk, and adapting compute, power, and control accordingly.
Keywords:Physical AI safety, vision-language-action models, inference latency,
robustness evaluation
1 Introduction
A language model that thinks longer gives a slower answer. A robot that thinks longer acts on an
older world. Consider the vision-language-action (VLA) modelπ 0.5 [1] asked to pick up a black
bowl next to a ramekin and place it on a plate (Fig. 1). Under its training viewpoint it succeeds
safely, and 400 ms of inference delay does not change that. With the camera rotated by60 ◦, it still
succeeds safely. Withboth, it sweeps across the table, knocking an identical, uninvolved bowl 19 cm
and pressing on the table with 201 N: a failure that neither condition causes alone. This is not an
anecdote: across 4,000 paired episodes, the effect of delay on safety grows sharply with viewpoint
shift (Sec. 3).
This example exposes two properties of physical AI safety. First,thresholds make additive effects
non-additive: physical shift and delay each consume safety margin, but harm occurs only when the
margin is exhausted, so conditions that are individually benign can be jointly unsafe. Second, the
system’s layers arecoupled: physical shift destabilizes the policy’s actions, and the conditions with
the least stable actions are those in which staleness costs the most.
Existing evaluations nonetheless examine these variations in isolation. Robustness benchmarks per-
turb viewpoint, lighting, and layout while assuming instantaneous inference [2]; real-time execution
methods mitigate inference delay under nominal scenes [3, 4, 5]; and safety benchmarks separate
success from safety while holding compute fixed [6, 7].

2 Cross-Layer Variation Propagation
Layers of the robot system.Bylayerwe mean a level of the robot system, not of the network:
the physicalenvironment, theembodiment, themodel, thecompute platform, andexecution(Fig. 2;
App. A). Safety outcomes—collisions, excessive contact force, disturbance of non-target objects—
emerge from how these interact in closed loop, and execution feeds back into the next observation.
Interaction.Delay matters only in context: 200 ms is negligible in a static, well-perceived scene
but decisive when the policy is uncertain or the scene is changing, so risk is not a function of delay
alone. LetR(E, C)be the probability of a safety violation during a rollout under physical-variation
levelEand compute conditionC, with(0,0)nominal. We define thecross-layer interaction
IE,C =R(E, C)−R(E,0)−R(0, C) +R(0,0).(1)
IE,C ≈0means the variations are additive and can be evaluated separately;I E,C >0means
their combination creates risk beyond the sum of its parts. In the example of Fig. 1, only the joint
condition fails, soI= 1−0−0 + 0 = 1. A cross-layer analysis locates such regimes and the
pathwaysthat produce them, e.g., shift→representation drift→action instability→stale execution
→unsafe contact; this pathway is hypothesized, and Sec. 3 measures only its action-instability step.

Related work.VLA robustness benchmarks show large drops under modest camera shifts [2] but
assume instantaneous inference. Real-time execution methods compensate for inference delay in
chunked policies [3, 4, 5], and adaptive test-time compute trades decision quality against latency [8];
both are evaluated in nominal scenes and by task success. Safety benchmarks measure violations
beyond success [6, 7], and failure detectors monitor model-internal signals [9, 10], but compute is
held fixed. Perturbing the reasoning stage exposes architecture-specific fragility [11]. We connect
these threads: the variations they study separately interact.
3 Preliminary Evidence

tion: the agent-view camera is orbited about the workspace by0 ◦,30 ◦,45 ◦, and60 ◦ (E0–E3). The
levels were chosen on held-out initial states so that zero-delay success spans 0.93 to 0.53 (details
in App. B).Compute variation: inference latencyδ∈ {0,50,100,200,400}ms. For reference, one
π0.5 chunk takes 221 ms on an RTX 3080, so this range brackets consumer-GPU latency. Because
simulation pauses during inference, we emulate latency in simulation time. Each chunk is computed
from the observationk=δ/∆tsteps old (∆t= 50ms) and executed from the index for the current
step, so every executed action is at leastksteps stale; one inference is in flight at a time. This is the

Delay is benign in distribution, not under shift.Under the training viewpoint, even 400 ms of
delay barely changes the outcome: success falls from 0.97 to 0.92 and the violation rate rises from
0.045 to 0.080 (Fig. 3a,b). Under a60 ◦ shift, the same delay drops success from 0.57 to 0.06 and
raises the violation rate from 0.215 to 0.585.

and delay (Fig. 3c). It is significantly positive in 6 of the 12 off-nominal cells, reachingI E,C =

+0.165(0.08–0.24) with 200 ms, which is close toπ 0.5’s own chunk latency on a consumer GPU.

4 Research Agenda: Toward Predictable Physical Intelligence
Where does variation amplify?Instrumenting every layer—representation drift, action variance,
latency, action staleness, and contact—turns a failure into a traceable pathway. The scientific target
is to identify where along the stack variation is amplified, and which layer gives the earliest warning.
App. F gives a protocol: log these signals every step and vary one layer at a time.
When does variation become risk?Most variation is harmless: a distribution shift or a latency
spike is not a failure. Rather than detecting anomalies, a runtime monitor should predict propagation,

extending model-internal failure detectors [9], such as SAFE [10], with compute and execution
state. Our pathway result suggests why: action instability alone does not identify which episodes a
delay will harm. Our grid, re-run with per-step logs, is a first testbed (App. F).
When should a robot think longer, and when should it act sooner?More test-time compute
can improve a decision while making it staler [8], so risk may be non-monotonic in compute. The
balance depends on scene dynamics, proximity to hazards, the precision the task requires, and un-
certainty: a difficult but slow-changing scene rewards deliberation, whereas a dynamic one rewards
fast, frequent updates. Injected delay captures only the cost of computation; its benefit must be
measured with real compute, such as more denoising steps or several sampled chunks (App. F).
Compute and power as safety variables.A power cap or thermal throttling lowers GPU fre-
quency, lengthens inference, and makes every action staler. Compute and power should therefore
be allocated by physical risk rather than for efficiency alone—for instance, minimizing energy sub-
ject toP(violation)≤ϵ—with responses ranging from more compute to shorter action horizons,
re-observation, slowing down, or stopping. Sweeping GPU power caps tests this (App. F).
Together, these questions definepredictable physical intelligence: embodied AI that stays pre-
dictable and safe despite variation across its environment, model, compute, and execution.
5 Conclusion and Limitations
Physical safety of robot foundation models is a cross-layer property: the conditions that matter
most are combinations, not isolated perturbations. In 4,000 paired episodes, inference delay that
is nearly harmless under the training viewpoint becomes a major source of unsafe behavior under
viewpoint shift. Our evidence is preliminary: one policy, one task suite, one perturbation pair, and
emulated delay in simulation. Extending to more policies and perturbations, to hardware-induced
delay (contention, power caps, thermal throttling), and to real robots is future work (App. G).

A Layers, Action Age, and the Interaction Contrast
Layers.Variation arises at every layer of the embodied stack: the physicalenvironment(lighting,
clutter, viewpoint, object pose, dynamics), theembodiment(calibration, payload, actuator response),
themodel(representation shift, action instability, uncertainty), thecompute platform(latency, jitter,
contention, power caps, thermal throttling), andexecution(stale actions, missed control updates,
trajectory deviation). Throughout, a layer is a level of the robot system, never a layer of the policy
network.
Action age.Letx t be the physical state ande t the environment condition. The policy observes
ot =H(x t, et) +ϵ t and, for instructionℓ, outputs an action chunk(a t, . . . , at+T−1 ) =π θ(ot, ℓ).
Inference takesδ t, so actions computed fromo t execute no earlier thant+δ t, when the state has
already evolved tox t+δt. We callA t =t exec −t obs theaction age. It is not the control period:
under chunked execution it is the inference delay plus the action’s position in its chunk. In our setup
(App. B), executed actions are 0–450 ms old without delay andδtoδ+ 450ms old with delayδ;
delay shifts the whole range.
Interaction contrast.Eq. (1) is the standard interaction contrast of a2×2design, a difference of
differences:I E,C = [R(E, C)−R(E,0)]−[R(0, C)−R(0,0)], i.e., how much more the compute
condition raises risk under physical variation than in the nominal scene. What is new is applying it
across layers.

margin it consumes exceeds a latent marginM, and physical and compute variation consume mar-

B Experimental Details
Policy and simulation.We use the public checkpointlerobot/pi05 libero finetuned v044
ofπ 0.5 fine-tuned on LIBERO, run in LeRobot 0.6.1 at its shipped precision (bf16, vision tower in
fp32) withouttorch.compile. To fit a 10 GB RTX 3080, we drop the two text-decoding heads,
which flow-matching action sampling does not use. The policy receives256×256agent-view and
wrist images and the robot state, controls at 20 Hz, predicts 50-step chunks, and replans everyr= 10
steps. An episode ends on success or after 280 steps (14 s). The grid uses LIBERO’s initial states
0–19 of each of the 10 LIBERO-Spatial tasks; calibration used the disjoint states 40–43.
Viewpoint shift.At every reset, the agent-view camera is rotated by yawθabout the vertical axis
through the point where its optical axis meets the table top, so it keeps looking at the same point;
the wrist camera is unchanged. We fixedE 1–E3 before running the grid, from a zero-delay sweep
on held-out initial states (Table 1): they are the tested angles whose success is closest to 85, 65, and
45%.
Latency emulation.The simulator pauses during inference, so we emulate a robot that keeps
moving while the policy computes. Withk=δ/∆t∈ {0,1,2,4,8}steps, the chunk computed from
the observation at steps i becomes active ats i +k; until then the previous chunk keeps running. One
inference is in flight at a time, so observations are taken everymax(r, k) = 10steps in every cell
and the inference cadence is the same for all delays. At steptthe robot executes indext−s i ≥k
of the active chunk, so every executed action is at leastδold, and no index beyond 17 of 50 is used.

The first chunk runs fromt= 0, while the robot is at rest. This is the naive asynchronous setting of
RTC and VLASH [3, 4], without their inpainting or future-state conditioning.
Safety instrumentation.Targets are the task’s objects of interest: the bowl and the plate. At each
reset, any non-target object touching a target is asupportand is excluded from the violation outcome.
Supports are the cookie box, the cabinet (drawer), the ramekin, the stove, and the cabinet (top) in
tasks 3, 4, 5, 7, and 9, and, in some initial states, the ramekin in task 0 and the other bowl in task 2.
At every step we record the normal force of each robot contact using MuJoCo’smjcontactForce,
and the displacement of every non-target, non-support object from its starting position. An episode
is aviolationwhen the maximum displacement exceedsD= 2cm or the maximum robot contact
force on a non-target object, a fixture, or the table exceedsF= 150N. Both were fixed before the
grid ran:Dmarks an object that was knocked or pushed, andFis 1.5 times the largest non-support
contact force in nominal calibration runs (101.6 N, against the table). A successful-but-unsafe (SBU)
episode both succeeds and violates.
Chunk inconsistency.At each replanning step, we compute the mean L2 distance, in the policy’s
normalized action space, between the new chunk and the previous chunk over the first 10 steps on
which they overlap, and average it over the episode. It is a proxy for action instability (cf. [9]).
Paired design.Every cell runs the same 200 (task, initial state) units. The policy’s sampling noise
is re-seeded at every inference from a fixed seed and the step index, and each unit keeps its batch
slot, so all cells share common random numbers and differ only in yaw and delay. This pairing
makes per-episode comparisons such as Fig. 1 possible.

C Statistical Analysis
Episode-level bootstrap.The resampling unit is a (task, initial state) pair, which appears once in
each of the 20 cells. Each of 4,000 resamples (seed 0) draws 200 units with replacement, without
stratifying by task, recomputes all 20 cell rates on the same units, and computesI E,C from them;
the 95% interval spans the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles. Because the four terms ofI E,C share units,
the interval accounts for the correlation that pairing induces. A cell is starred in Fig. 3c when its
interval excludes 0.
Task-level bootstrap.Units from the same task are not independent, so we also resample the 10
tasks with replacement, keeping all 20 units of each (4,000 resamples). This widens the intervals
but leaves the same six cells significantly positive (Table 2). The small negative interaction at (30◦,
50 ms) is significant only at the episode level.
Consistency across tasks.Table 3 givesI E,C for each task separately. At 400 ms, the violation
interaction is positive in all 10 tasks at60 ◦ (sign testp= 0.002) and in 9 of 10 at45 ◦, the tenth
being exactly zero (p= 0.004). The success interaction is negative in 9 of 10 tasks in each of the
four cells shown. Its size varies across tasks, from+0.05to+0.70at (60 ◦, 400 ms), which App. F
proposes to relate to task precision and clearance.
Multiple comparisons.We test the 12 off-nominal cells without correction. The six significantly
positive cells are not scattered: they form the high-shift, high-delay corner of the grid (≥45 ◦,
≥100ms), and all six survive the more conservative task-level bootstrap.

Does instability predict harm from delay?Table 5 splits each viewpoint’s episodes into terciles
of zero-delay chunk inconsistency and compares their change from 0 to 400 ms. Across viewpoints,
mean instability and harm from delay rise together. Within a viewpoint they do not: pooled over
viewpoints, the most unstable third gains 0.19 in violation rate, against 0.16 and 0.25 for the other
thirds, and loses the least success (0.20, against 0.46 and 0.50). Instability thus explains the trend
across viewpoints but not which episodes a delay will harm.
Hypotheses stated before the grid ran.Our design document fixed three hypotheses before the
grid ran. H1, that unsafe success grows with shift and delay, is not supported: it stays at 0.5–5% in
every cell. H2, thatI E,C >0under moderate and severe shift at delays that are benign atE 0, is
supported (Table 2). H3, that harm from delay scales with shift-induced chunk inconsistency, holds
across viewpoints but not within them (Table 5).

E Relation to LIBERO-Plus
LIBERO-Plus [2] perturbs seven factors—object layout, camera viewpoint, robot initial state, lan-
guage instruction, lighting, background texture, and sensor noise—and finds VLAs extremely sensi-
tive to camera viewpoint and robot initial state. It also measures a compositional generalization gap:
combined scene perturbations hurt more than their individual effects predict. Both findings concern
task success under instantaneous inference.
We build on these results rather than repeat them. We take viewpoint as our scene variable be-
cause LIBERO-Plus shows VLAs are highly sensitive to it, and our zero-delay results reproduce
that sensitivity forπ 0.5: success falls from 0.97 at0 ◦ to 0.57 at60 ◦. What we add is (i) a second
variable from a different layer, inference delay, which scene benchmarks hold at zero; (ii) safety
outcomes—violations, unsafe success, and violations among failures—rather than success alone;
and (iii) a paired design in which every cell runs the same episodes with the same sampling noise, so
the interaction is estimated within episodes, with bootstrap intervals at the episode and task levels.

F Research Plan
Where does variation amplify?Measurements:at every control step, one signal per layer—
camera pose and scene state (environment); the distance of the policy’s vision-language embedding
from its value for the same state under the nominal view, and chunk inconsistency (model); inference
latency and jitter (compute); action age (execution); and contact forces and object displacement
(outcome).Protocol:starting from the paired grid of Sec. 3, vary one layer at a time, then pairs of
layers, on the same episodes.Evaluation:for each signal, the fraction of violations it precedes by
leaving its nominal range (the central 95% under nominal conditions) and its lead time; and, for each
pair of layers, the interaction contrast of App. A, which shows where variation is amplified rather
than passed on.
When does variation become risk?Data:our grid re-run with per-step logging, which labels
each step by whether a violation follows withinHsteps, plus new grids with other perturbations.
Model:a monitorr t on the cross-layer featureszt above, compared with a detector on model features
only, as in SAFE [10], and with chunk inconsistency alone.Evaluation:leave-one-task-out and
leave-one-viewpoint-out splits, so that the monitor is tested on unseen conditions; AUROC, false-
alarm rate at fixed recall, and warning lead time; and conformal calibration of the alarm threshold,
as in SAFE. Sec. 3 predicts that compute and execution features help most where model features are
weakest: telling which episodes a delay will harm.
When should a robot think longer, and when should it act sooner?Injected delay (Sec. 3)
captures only the cost of computation; to capture its benefit, thinking longer should be implemented
with real compute—more denoising steps, sampling several chunks and keeping the most consistent,
or a larger model—and evaluated by its safety gain against its staleness cost in slow and dynamic
scenes.Protocol:run each compute setting in real time, so that its measured latency is the delay,
in static scenes, in scenes with moving objects, and with hazards at varying distances from the
target.Evaluation:success, violations, andI E,C as functions of the compute budget; risk that is
non-monotonic in compute would confirm that the best budget depends on the scene.
Compute and power as safety variables.Protocol:on real hardware, such as a desktop GPU and
an embedded module, sweep power caps and locked clock frequencies, with and without thermal
load, and record per-chunk latency, jitter, energy, and violations on the same paired episodes.Eval-
uation:fit risk as a function of power; then compare a risk-aware allocator that minimizes energy
subject toP(violation)≤ϵ—adding compute, shortening action horizons, re-observing, slowing
down, or stopping—with fixed-power baselines, by the energy saved at equal violation rate.
Task precision and clearance.Per-task interactions vary widely (Table 3). Relating them to task
properties—the initial clearance between the target and the nearest non-target object, and the place-
ment tolerance—and then varying clearance systematically in new initial states would test whether
sensitivity to delay depends on the precision a task requires and on the distance to obstacles.
G Discussion of Open Questions
Are viewpoint shift and latency in the training data?Not in the fine-tuning data: the LIBERO
demonstrations thatπ 0.5 was fine-tuned on use a fixed agent-view camera pose and synchronous
control, so no executed action in them is stale. We cannot inspectπ 0.5’s pre-training mixture; what-
ever viewpoint diversity it contains did not prevent the drop we measure after fine-tuning.
Would fine-tuning on these conditions remove the interaction?Viewpoint augmentation, and
delay-aware training or execution such as RTC’s inpainting [3] or VLASH’s future-state condition-
ing [4], should shrink each main effect. Whether they remove the interaction is open. The threshold
argument of App. A suggests they will shrink it rather than eliminate it: as long as both conditions

Would describing the conditions in the instruction help?π 0.5 is conditioned on language, but
its instructions describe tasks, not camera poses or latency. An instruction such as “the camera is
rotated by60 ◦” is therefore itself out of distribution, and we expect little benefit without training on
such descriptions. Adding these prompts to the grid would test this cheaply.
Other perturbations.Viewpoint and delay are one pair. Lighting, clutter, sensor noise, dropped
frames, actuator lag, and latency jitter are natural next pairs; each costs one more grid of the same
paired design.
Emulated delay.Our emulation holds latency fixed within a cell. Real systems add jitter and
occasional long stalls, which the hardware sweeps above would expose.
