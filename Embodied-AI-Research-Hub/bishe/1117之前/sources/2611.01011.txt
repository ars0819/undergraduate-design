Leap-VLA: High-Rate Robot Control
from a Low-Rate VLM Backbone
Lin Yang*, Yunjie Shen, Zheyu Li, Zhenjie Tian, Yongli Wang, Dongze Huang,
Weiqi Wang, Yucheng Zhu, Zhaoquan Jiang, Guofeng Xie, Peigen Fang
Embodied Algorithm Group, Automation PBU, Leapmotor.
*Corresponding author: Lin Yang.
Abstract—Vision-language-action (VLA) models re-encode the
full observation through a multi-billion-parameter vision–
language model (VLM) at every control step, even though scene
semantics evolve at roughly 1 Hz while reactive control requires
20–50 Hz. We present Leap-VLA, a VLA that decouples the
two: a VLM runs as a low-frequencysemantic anchorwhose
cached contextis reused across control steps, while a flow-
matching action expert operates at high frequency, consuming
the cached semantics with a fresh, low-latency visual stream
from a compact encoder pretrained on robot video with masked
reconstruction, optical flow, and depth. Themeasuredageτof the
cache conditions the expert through adaptive layer normalization.
Reusing the cache makes the policy a function of its age, and
training covers that function explicitly: cache ages sampled from
real episode history, plus an age-ramped pixel corruption that
erases the stale snapshot the way deployed staleness corrupts
content. On the asynchronous deployment stack, the recipe closes
most of the staleness gap—on LIBERO-Spatial, 61.4%→95.5%
at a 2 s semantic refresh (95.9% at 1 s, within 1.6 points of the
frequency-matched anchor); averaged over the four LIBERO
suites, the deployed policy holds 95.2% at a 1 s refresh and
94.5% at 2 s against a 96.3% matched anchor. A forward-only
chunk-boundary correction (soft-nudge), an autograd-free refor-
mulation of real-time-chunking guidance, carries per-control-step
re-planning without loss (97.1%) at zero added latency. The
same lens exposes a second defect: under an instruction–scene
mismatch the policy keeps manipulating the scene, and a chain-
of-thought verdict gate trained on zero-cost negative episodes
freezes execution exactly on mismatched instructions without
hurting feasible success.
I. INTRODUCTION
Vision-language-action models map camera observations
and language instructions directly to robot actions, and recent
systems built on pretrained VLMs have demonstrated strong
generalization across tasks, objects, and scenes [2], [3], [4],
[5]. A structural property shared by essentially all of these
systems is that semantic understanding and action generation
runin lockstep: at every re-planning step the full observation
is re-encoded by the VLM backbone, and only then does the
action head run.
This coupling is mismatched to the statistics of manipula-
tion. Scene semantics—which objects are present, where they
roughly are, what the task requires—change at about 1 Hz and
are often quasi-static for seconds at a time. Reactive control, in
contrast, must respond to proprioceptive drift, contact events,
and short-horizon object motion at 20–50 Hz. Running a multi-
billion-parameter VLM at control frequency wastes the vast
majority of its compute on observations that are semantically
identical to the previous step, and it caps the re-planning rate
at whatever the slow system affords. The mismatch becomes
prohibitive as backbones scale or as deployment moves to edge
hardware.
The obvious fix—reuse the VLM’s processed features
across control steps and re-run only the action head—fails
in two specific ways. First, the cached prefix goesstale:
it describes a past state of the world, and in current VLA
designs it typically also embeds therobot state as textin the
prompt, so the cache actively contradicts the fresh observation
as soon as the robot moves. We measure this on the deployed
asynchronous stack: a policy fine-tuned at the semantic refresh
rate reaches 97.5%, but the same policy trained synchronously
and then deployed with the semantic loop slowed to a 2 s
refresh collapses to 61.4% on LIBERO-Spatial (Sec. IV-C).
Second, even with a fresh cache, an action head that only sees
semantics cannot be reactive: short-horizon correction requires
fresh, low-level, spatially grounded input at the same rate as
the control loop.
Leap-VLA addresses both failures with three ingredients.
(i) Age-conditioned cache reuse.The expert is conditioned
on themeasuredageτof the cache (the time since the
last refresh) alongside the flow time and the current propri-
oceptive state. Training samples the cache age∆from the
physical episode history, so the policy sees the full staleness
distribution it will encounter at deployment, including the
contradiction between stale semantics and fresh geometry that
it must learn to arbitrate.(ii) A fresh visual stream on
the fast clock.All fast-moving visual information enters the
expert as a pluggable token block—appended to the expert’s
input sequence and attended by its self-attention—and is
refreshed wholesale at every re-planning step: motion-aware
image tokens from a frozen encoder pretrained on robot video
(masked reconstruction, optical flow, and depth, so inter-frame
motion and geometry are learned exactly where the stream is
consumed). Slower-reading signals attach through gated cross-
attention with zero-initialized output projections; the cache age
τenters through adaptive layer normalization (AdaRMS) and
proprioception as a dedicated expert input token.(iii) A chain-
of-thought-supervised anchor.The slow backbone carries
a short chain-of-thought region appended after the prompt;
because attention is causal, the features the expert consumes
are unchanged by the appended region, so the reasoning shapes
what the anchor knows without entering the action path—and

II. RELATEDWORK
A. Vision-Language-Action Models
VLA models inherit pretrained VLMs [21], [22] and fine-
tune them for control, either by decoding actions as text [1],
[2], by attaching regression heads [3], or by generative action
decoders [4], [5], [7].π 0 [4] popularized the architecture
we build on: a VLM prefix (frozen inπ 0) and a smaller
flow-matching action expert coupled throughshared attention,
where every expert layer attends the KV of the corresponding
VLM layer.π 0.5 [5] extends this with open-world general-
ization. All of these systems re-run the full VLM at every
re-planning step; none conditions on the age of its semantic
context.
B. Hierarchical and Dual-Rate Robot Policies
Several systems decouple slow reasoning from fast control.
Hi Robot [9] lets a VLM restructure user commands for a
fast low-level policy. Helix [10] runs a 7B VLM at 7–9 Hz
and an 80M visuomotor policy at 200 Hz, communicating
through a single fixed-size latent vector. G0 [11] couples a
VLM planner with a separately trained VLA executor—two
full models, each carrying its own vision–language backbone,
joined through subtask instructions. RoboDual [12] and latent-
bridge approaches [13] distill a generalist’s output into a
specialist’s conditioning; Fast-in-Slow [14] repurposes the
VLM’s final blocks as the fast system, which consumes the
slow system’s latest latent regardless of its age. These works
build the dual system bycomposingmodels: two separately
parameterized, separately trained systems whose fast side sees
the slow one only through language or a fixed latent that
bottlenecks what crosses. Leap-VLA insteadsplitsa single
VLA into its two loops: the slow and fast loops are the
two halves of one shared-attention model, coupled layer-wise
through the full cached context, and the staleness the split
introduces is madeexplicit and learnablethrough measured-
age conditioning rather than hidden behind a fixed latent. To
our knowledge, no prior VLA work conditions its action expert
on the measured age of its semantic context or studies success
as a function of that age.
C. Latency, Chunking, and Asynchrony
Action chunking with temporal ensembling [8] smooths
execution across overlapping chunks; real-time chunking [16]
mitigates inference latency at deployment by inpainting actions
that will execute while the next chunk is computed. These
are inference-time remedies orthogonal to ours: they assume a
policy queried at high rate, which is exactly what Leap-VLA’s
fast expert enables.
D. Structured Reasoning in VLA
Embodied chain-of-thought [15] interleaves free-text rea-
soning with control, placing explicit reasoning text on the
inference critical path. We instead co-train the chain-of-
thought with the control objective: the reasoning capability
is internalized in the anchor during training, and explicit
CoT generation at deployment is optional. Our slow backbone
carries a short chain-of-thought region trained with a language-
modeling objective; causal attention keeps the expert’s input
features bitwise identical, so the reasoning shapes the anchor’s
representation without entering the action path, and a verdict
head built on the same supervision gates execution under
instruction–scene mismatch (Sec. V).
E. Self-Supervised Visual Pretraining for Control
Robot-domain visual pretraining distills web-scale self-
supervision into control-friendly features, from stage-one
objectives (R3M [30], V oltron [31]) to masked autoen-
coding [27]. Our fast-stream encoder follows the masked-
autoencoder recipe but adds explicitinter-frame motionand
geometryobjectives—dense optical flow against a frozen

III. METHOD
A. Overview
Leap-VLA separates a VLA policy into aslow loopat
frequencyf slow and afast loopat frequencyf fast, withf slow ≪
ffast. The slow loop runs a Qwen2.5-VL-2B [21] VLMΦ—
initialized from the pretrained checkpoint and, depending on
the task, kept frozen, adapted with LoRA, or fully fine-tuned—
that encodes the instruction, a camera observation, and its own
short autoregressive output, and produces thecached context
C: the features it has processed for the current scene prefix,
held fixed until the next refresh. The concrete form ofC
is exchangeable—per-layer key–value pairs read by shared
attention, or backbone features read by cross-attention—and
nothing downstream depends on which. The fast loop runs
a flow-matching action expertE θ that, at every control step,
denoises an action chunk while attending toCand to freshly
encoded fast-stream features (motion-aware image tokens),
all entering through one pluggable token-block interface. The
only quantity coupling the two loops besidesCis the measured
cache ageτ. Fig. 1 illustrates the architecture.
B. Slow System: The Semantic Anchor
1) Prompt and cache:At refresh timet r, the VLM prefix
contains the language instruction and the current camera
observation. Crucially, the robot state isnotpart of the prompt:
embedding state as text in a cached prefix is self-defeating,
since the cached copy becomes wrong the moment the robot
moves. State enters the fast loop instead (Sec. III-C). The pre-
fix therefore stays semantically valid under cache reuse: scene
layout and task identity, not instantaneous proprioception.
2) Chain-of-thought supervision:The backbone is a causal
language model, and its structured output is a shortchain-
of-thought(CoT) region appended after the prompt: a fixed
set of fields, generated autoregressively and supervised with a
language-modeling (cross-entropy) loss through an untied LM
head. Because attention is causal and positions are relative,
the prompt-region features that the action head consumes
are bitwise identical with and without the appended block:
CoT supervision shapes what the anchorknows—the features
it hands the expert—without adding anything to the action
path, and no CoT needs to be generated at deployment unless
its output is consumed. We use the region in two roles: as
training-side feature supervision, and—through a verdict head
trained on the same supervision—as a deployment-side gate
under instruction–scene mismatch (Sec. V).
3) Cache refresh policy:The cache is refreshed (i) period-
ically everyT ref seconds, (ii) on trigger events—large frame
difference or optical flow at the image level, gripper open/close
events, or subtask changes—and (iii) on episode reset. The
reported deployments exercise (i) and (iii) on the request-gated
schedule of Sec. IV-A; the trigger path is supported by the
design but not exercised in any reported cell. Refreshes are
executed asynchronously; the control loop never blocks on
the VLM. Each control step reads themeasuredcache age
τ=t−t refresh, which accounts for real scheduling jitter.
The fast-stream encoder (Sec. III-D) is pretrained offline
and frozen at deployment; the VLM starts from the pretrained
checkpoint and is kept frozen, adapted with LoRA, or fully
fine-tuned as the task requires. The slow loop’s runtime jobs
are the periodic refresh and, when consumed, the structured
output, all amortized over many control steps.

expert tokens attend the cached contextCand each other, with
the concrete form of the coupling left open (per-layer shared
attention as inπ 0, or cross-attention to the cached features).

a scalar conditioning vector for everything scalar, and a set

D. Motion-Aware Pretraining of the Fast Visual Stream
The fast loop needs a visual encoder of its own, and three
requirements separate it from the VLM’s vision tower: it
must run every re-planning cycle (compact, launch-cheap);
its features must bespatially dense and motion-informative
rather than language-aligned semantics (they are consumed by
the expert’s cross-attention, not by a language model); and it
must see the robot’s own visual domain. We therefore pretrain
a compact encoder on multi-source robot video, then freeze it
into the image modality of Sec. III-C.
1) Architecture:A∼23M-parameter encoder: a convolu-
tional stem with four downsampling stages followed by three
wide transformer layers with rotary position embeddings,
so the positional encoding is resolution-agnostic (the same
weights run at reduced resolution for ablations). Each sample
is adual-frame pair(o t−δ, ot)whose two frames are fused
into a 6-channel input at the stem (early fusion), yielding196
tokens per camera at224 2/patch-16; the deployed geometry
is identical to the pretraining geometry, so no representation
shift is introduced at deployment.
2) Objectives: reconstruction, motion, and depth:Three
masked-autoencoder-style [27] objectives share the encoder
(Fig. 2). (i)Masked reconstruction:∼49%of patches (a
row/column strip mask) are held out, and a lightweight decoder
reconstructsbothframes of the pair from the merged features.
(ii)Inter-frame motion: a flow decoder predicts, for every
masked patch, the dense optical flowo t−δ →o t in pixels,
supervised by per-pixel end-point error against ground truth
generated in the data loader by a frozen off-the-shelf flow
teacher (W AFT [28]). Flow supervision penetrates the same
merged features the reconstruction uses, so the encoder is
explicitly taughtwhat changed between the two frames—
the quantity the reactive loop cares about. (iii)Depth: a
depth decoder predicts, for every masked patch, the per-pixel
depth of each frame of the pair, supervised by per-pixel

Training uses the standard flow-matching objective of [4],

VLM is likewise kept frozen, so can the cached prefix features.
This both speeds training and permits full-precision expert
optimization without the memory footprint of the backbones.
At inference, each environment holds its own cache; a re-
planning cycle runs the prefix, encodes the fresh fast stream
once (its features are reused across all Euler steps), then
denoises with the measuredτ; (optionally) overlapping chunks
are fused by temporal voting [8], [16].

D. Chunk-Boundary Continuity and Forward-Only Correction
Success rates hide a failure mode of chunked execution:
when a new chunk replaces the one being executed, its
first executed action can jump. With 0.5 s re-planning these
boundaries arrive twice a second, and on the uncorrected
asynchronous stack the boundary displacement averages1.9×
the mean intra-chunk displacement (Table VI)—a visible jerk
exactly when the semantic loop refreshes.

The gain of decoupling, on the deployment stack.Decou-

the semantic encoding runs—and the effect lands directly on
the serving path. Waiting for one action request costs the
same-frequency system 73.9 ms (semantic refresh plus fast
loop, serialized on the critical path) and the decoupled system
26.7 ms:2.8×per request. Counting the semantic cost at its
amortized share (11.8 ms per fast request at the 1:4 ratio), per-
request compute drops from 73.9 to 38.5 ms (1.9×), and the
re-planning clock a single stream can serve extends from 13.5
to 26 Hz. Semantics stop being a per-action prerequisite and
become a background expense amortized at1/f slow.
Feasibility, not speed.The advantage concentrates at the
reactive end. Serving the most reactive column of Table V—
a fresh plan every 50 ms control step, each plan executing
a single action under that column’s 2 s semantic refresh—
the same-frequency joint occupies the GPU at 148%: past
100%, the control loop cannot close in real time, so this clock
is undeployable for the same-frequency layout on hardware,
whatever its simulated success (26.9). The decoupled schedule
occupies 56%: the only configuration that serves the reactive
column in real time, and precisely the column where the
asynchronous policy holds 91.4 (97.1 with soft-nudge).
Fast-stream scaling.The fast stream is not the bottleneck:
encodingVwrist-camera frame pairs costs 1.3/1.8/2.3 ms at

the denoise cycle, and the boundary-correction variants of

F . Zero-Shot Robustness on LIBERO-plus
We submit the deployed system to LIBERO-plus [18],
which extends the four LIBERO suites to 10,030 perturbed
task variants across seven dimensions—camera view, robot
embodiment, language, lighting, background, camera noise,
object layout—graded over five difficulty levels, one trial
per task. The evaluated checkpoint is the shared four-suite
LIBERO model (600k steps), trained only on the original
LIBERO suites, so every perturbation dimension is zero-shot;
it is served by the same asynchronous deployment server as
the deployment studies (re-planning every 0.5 s, no boundary
correction). Table VIII places the result on the benchmark’s
leaderboard. At 69.2% total, the model lands in the first tier of
the group trained without LIBERO-plus data (OpenVLA-OFT
69.6, ahead ofπ 0-Fast 61.6); it leads the robot-embodiment
dimension outright (68.4%) and leads camera view (77.2%)
and camera noise (82.6%) within that group; per suite it scores
81.0% (object), 68.9% (spatial), 68.8% (goal), and 58.0%
(LIBERO-10), degrading from 80.4% at difficulty level 1 to
53.3% at level 5. The dimension profile is complementary
to the baselines’: lighting (53.5%) and background (55.6%)
trail the strongest baselines by 35–40 points—the signature
of a policy whose visual grounding is geometric and motion-
based but overfit to the photometric statistics of its training
distribution—exactly the dual of the baselines’ weakness in
camera view and embodiment. Figure 4 shows one successful
level-5 episode on each of the three strongest dimensions;
Appendix D illustrates the characteristic failures of the two
weakest.

H. Real-Robot Results
We deploy Leap-VLA on four platforms (Fig. 6): two dual-
arm workstations (Realman, Galaxea), the Dobot Atom mobile
dual-arm humanoid, and our in-house humanoid upper body.
All tasks run from the same onboard camera layout as training,
with no per-task tuning; Table X reports per-task success.
Protocol.Each task is evaluated for 50 trials on its platform.
A trial succeeds when the task’s end condition holds: blocks
grouped into two color-pure piles; the carton packed and
closed; all four workpieces in the side cartons; the workpiece
resting in the target bin; colored workpieces sorted with the
distractor left out; the two letter rows completed; and the
bottle standing upright inside the marked target zone. Same-
freq. runs the full VLM at every control step; Leap-VLA runs
the asynchronous stack of Sec. IV-C—semantic refresh every
four control steps (1:4), with the fresh fast stream and the
measured cache age updated at every control step. In the
interactive task a human only hands over and re-stands the
bottle between autonomous cycles; trials are scored on the
autonomous segments.
V. DOVLASKNOWWHEN THESCENEDOESN’TMATCH
THEINSTRUCTION?
Every VLA in this paper’s lineage is trained the same
way, and the way is itself a defect: the training distribution
consists entirely of successful demonstrations, so the policy is
never asked whether the instruction issatisfiable—P(action|
obs,instr)is sampled only where it is — and the action head
has no idle or abstain mode. The result is anachievability
prior: under an instruction–scene mismatch the policy does
not idle, it keeps manipulating the scene — grasping unrelated
objects that happen to be present, knocking over obstacles
(Fig. 8) — because the language-to-action shortcut bypasses
visual verification altogether. This is the same training-side
gap that leaves the leftover conflict of Appendix B unresolved:
in both cases the supervision never contains a single sample
whose correct response to “the observation disagrees with what
the instruction (or prefix) says” is to stop.
Zero-cost negatives.We close part of the gap with a data
intervention that costs no extra collection: re-assign instruc-
tions from the other LIBERO suites to goal-scene episodes
and keep a pair only when at least one referenced object is
absent (the simulator’s bddl object list acts as the geometric
ground truth), labeled with the zero action (idle). This yields
240 episodes over 27 mismatched prompts (120 pairs), 23%
of fine-tuning exposure, trained for 15k steps from the same
base checkpoint — no teleoperated failures, no environment
modification, no new loss terms.

VI. LIMITATIONS
Leap-VLA trains and evaluates staleness up to∆ max; ages
beyond the trained range are not covered, and triggering-
based refreshes trade compute for smaller effective ages rather
than extending the range. Even within the trained range,
measured-age conditioning and the corruption curriculum re-
cover deployed success but do not create information: between
refreshes the system holds no representation of what has hap-
pened since. Inference-time boundary corrections (Sec. IV-D)
smooth the deployment-typical self-leftover regime but cannot
force execution to follow a leftover that genuinely conflicts
with the fresh observation (Appendix B); closing that gap
requires training-side prefix-conflict adaptation, and our initial
attempts (Appendix C) did not close it. Finally, our evalua-
tion is confined to quasi-static simulation suites and a small
real-robot set; broader dynamic benchmarks are needed to
substantiate the reactivity claims at scale. The fast-stream
encoder is frozen; end-to-end finetuning of it—and the drift
this would cause in the learned stale-versus-fresh arbitration—
is unexplored.
VII. CONCLUSION
Leap-VLA turns a VLA’s semantic backbone from a per-
step bottleneck into a low-frequency anchor, and pays for
the resulting staleness explicitly: the expert is conditioned
on the measured age of its semantic cache and trained over
the full staleness distribution, while the fresh visual stream—
motion-aware image tokens—enters the expert through one
pluggable token-block interface and carries the reactive burden
at control rate. The result is a policy that preserves its
fresh anchor, matches same-frequency inference, and scales to
backbones and hardware where joint-frequency inference is in-
feasible. The deployed form of the stack is fully forward-only:
staleness-aware training carries the distribution, a gradient-
free boundary correction carries the chunk seams, and neither
pays an autograd or latency tax, so the control path exports to
compiled runtimes unchanged.
Scope and transfer.This is an architecture-level study: it
asks what a shared-attention VLA loses when its semantic loop
slows, and answers with single-variable training measurements
rather than leaderboard entries, so comparisons are restricted to
the VLA class under one deployment protocol. World-action
models, which couple action generation to predicted future
world states, optimize a different axis and are not compared.
The dual-rate systems of Sec. II compose separately trained
models whose fast side consumes the slow one only through
language or a fixed latent, and none characterizes the staleness
distribution its fast loop faces—the gap the ladder of Sec. IV-C
measures directly. The ingredients are portable by construc-
tion: asynchronous-∆texposure, measured-age conditioning,
the corruption curriculum, and soft-nudge modify only the
training distribution and the inference-time action update, so
they apply to any VLA that re-runs its backbone at every re-
planning step—demonstrated here at a 2B semantic backbone
(the compared rows span0.9–7B). We believe measured-age
conditioning is a general tool for any architecture that reuses
context across control steps, and we release our asynchronous
evaluation protocol to make such evaluations standard.

APPENDIXA
ASYNCHRONOUSEVALUATIONPROTOCOL
All deployment-line measurements (Tables III–VI) run the
same physical-clock loop, which reproduces the deployed
queue semantics in simulation. The client executes one action
per 50 ms. Control steps are grouped into re-planning windows
ofR=10(0.5 s;R=1in the0.05s columns of Table V);
within anR=10window, the observation for the next request
is captured at stepK=6and the request is issued, carrying the
leftover: the not-yet-executed tail of the currently executing
chunk, in raw action space, together with the number of
already-executed steps. When the window ends, the returned
chunk is adopted with its firstR−K=4steps discarded—
exactly the control steps that elapse while the chunk is
computed—and the remaining steps execute over the next
window. The first window has no leftover. The consumption
schedule is bit-identical across all compared corrections; the
only difference between systems is what the server does with
the leftover.
Staleness accounting.Every cell records, per request,
whether it took a fresh context or reused the cache, and the age
of the context it used. These sentries must match the scheduled
regime exactly—refresh fraction1.00and cache age≡0at
the matched anchors (the DiT:VLM1:1operating point of
Sec. IV-A); the alternating/cycling age patterns of Table IV at
the2:1/4:1cells—and every reported cell passes this check.
Boundary metric.For each adopted chunk we record the 6-
DoF displacement norm of its first executed step (the boundary
step) and the intra-window mean displacement; their ratio
(boundary/intra) aggregates per episode. RTC guidance uses
the exponential prefix schedule with execution horizonH=10
and maximum guidance weightγ=10; soft-nudge uses the
same schedule withη=1(Eq. 1); Figs. 9 and 10 show the
highest-contrast windows and the per-episode distributions.
