PACE: Phase-Aware Chunk Execution for Robot
Policies with Action Chunking
Junnan Nie1,2, Jiayi Li2, Chenghao Liu1, Junyi Lao1,
Jiachen Zhang1, Tianle Zhang2, Songfang Huang1

Abstract
Action-chunking robot policies predict sequences of future actions, but deployment
requires choosing how many actions to execute before replanning. The execution
horizon trades off feedback frequency against motion continuity. However, success
varies non-monotonically with the execution horizon, and the best-performing
horizon differs across tasks. These effects make a single fixed horizon unreliable as
a default execution strategy across tasks. We propose PACE (Phase-Aware Chunk
Execution), a training-free method that selects execution horizons online from
predicted action chunks. PACE uses prominent low-speed valleys in the predicted
speed profile as candidate replanning boundaries, exploiting the phase-dependent
kinematics of manipulation trajectories. By default, PACE calibrates the valley
threshold from each task’s existing training demonstrations, without task-specific
fixed-horizon tuning. On the 50-task RoboTwin2.0 benchmark, PACE improves
average success from 57.8% to 64.2% over the strongest fixed-horizon baseline and
outperforms the adaptive execution baselines AAC and AutoHorizon. With GR00T
N1.7 on LIBERO, PACE achieves the highest overall success rate among the
evaluated strategies, supporting its applicability across base policies, benchmarks,
and action representations. On real robots, PACE improves the average task score
from 60.7 to 77.7 and success rate from 50.7% to 70.4% over full-chunk execution.
Code is available on our project page.
1 Introduction
Recent progress in vision-language-action models and diffusion-based robot policies has largely come
from training-side gains. Larger backbones, broader demonstration data, and stronger multi-modal
pretraining have all improved predictive performance [1–7]. At deployment, however, performance
depends not only on how accurately a policy predicts, but also on how those predictions are executed.
To improve local motion continuity, these policies often adopt action chunking [4, 8], where each
query predicts a short sequence of future actions rather than a single-step command.
Yet an action chunk only specifies what the policy predicts, not how much of that prediction the
robot should execute. Its length fixes the prediction horizon, but a separate choice must still be
made about how many actions to execute before acquiring a new observation and querying the policy
again. We refer to this executed prefix length as theexecution horizon. Unlike the prediction horizon,
the execution horizon is neither learned nor prescribed by the policy [4, 8]. It is an inference-time
choice that trades off feedback frequency against within-chunk motion continuity. As such, the
execution horizon is not a mere implementation detail, but a central variable for reliable deployment
of action-chunking robot policies.

In practice, the execution horizon is typically fixed to a constant H throughout an episode [4, 8]. At
each replanning step, the robot executes the firstH actions of the predicted chunk before querying the
policy again, tying all chunks to the same replanning interval regardless of the motion they predict. A
smaller H incorporates new observations more often but can interrupt a coherent local motion, while
a larger H follows the predicted chunk longer but delays reactive correction. Without a principled
selection criterion,His usually chosen empirically.
Sweeping H from 1 to 50 on three RoboTwin2.0 tasks reveals strongly non-monotonic success rates
and task-dependent preferred horizons (Fig. 1, left). On Click Bell, success peaks near H≈6 , drops
by nearly 40 points around H≈25 , and rebounds to a second peak near H≈35 . A fixed horizon
can therefore cause substantial performance degradation on some tasks. The best horizon found
offline on one task also does not transfer to new tasks or real-robot deployment. These findings
indicate that the execution horizon must be selected online rather than fixed in advance.
We propose PACE (Phase-Aware Chunk Execution), a test-time method that selects the execution
horizon online from the speed profile of each predicted action chunk. Manipulation trajectories
consist of locally coherent motion segments, with transitions often accompanied by deceleration.
Policies trained via imitation learning preserve this structure in their predicted action chunks, where
phase transitions can appear as low-speed valleys in the speed profile. PACE treats such valleys
as candidate replanning points and executes the chunk up to the earliest accepted valley, aiming to
preserve coherent motion within a segment and re-query the policy before the next begins. PACE
uses no model-internal signals or learned components and requires no policy changes or retraining.
On the 50-task RoboTwin2.0 benchmark [9], PACE improves the average success rate ofπ0.5 from
57.8% to 64.2% over the best fixed-horizon baseline and outperforms the adaptive baselines AAC
and AutoHorizon. On real robots, it improves average task score from60.7to77.7and success rate
from 50.7% to 70.4% over full-chunk execution. With GR00T N1.7 on LIBERO, PACE achieves the
highest overall success rate among the evaluated strategies, supporting its applicability across policies,
benchmarks, and action representations. Ablations, sensitivity analyses, and fixed-horizon sweeps on
representative tasks assess its design choices, while analyses of successful and failed rollouts reveal
its working mechanism and failure modes. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We identify the execution horizon as a critical yet overlooked test-time variable, and show
empirically that its effect on success is both task-dependent and non-monotonic, indicating
that no single fixed horizon provides a reliable default across tasks.

2 Related work
Robot policies with action chunking.Action chunking has become a common interface in robot
policy learning. It was popularized by ACT for fine-grained bimanual imitation learning [ 8] and
Diffusion Policy for receding-horizon action-sequence generation [4]. Subsequent work applies this
interface to mobile manipulation [10], 3D visuomotor control [11], and large-scale bimanual policy
learning [12]. Generalist policies such as Octo, π0, and π0.5 extend chunk prediction to diverse tasks
and environments [3, 13, 14]. VLA research also explores efficient action tokenization [15] and faster
inference for action prediction [ 16, 17]. While these works focus on generating, representing, or
scaling action chunks, PACE addresses the complementary deployment-time question of how much
of each predicted chunk to execute before incorporating new observations and replanning.
Test-time execution of action chunks.Many policies execute action chunks using fixed test-
time rules: ACT smooths overlapping predictions through temporal ensembling, while Diffusion
Policy executes a fixed action horizon in a receding-horizon loop [ 4, 8]. Recent inference-time
methods reduce latency-induced discontinuities or stabilize chunk execution [18, 19]. Another line
modifies training or model design to reason over multiple horizons [20]. More closely related are
methods that adapt the execution horizon at inference time. Adaptive Action Chunking (AAC) [21]
estimates prefix-wise action entropy from multiple sampled chunks and selects the horizon subject to a
minimum-movement constraint. AutoHorizon [22] estimates the execution horizon from action-token
self-attention in flow-based VLAs, treating attention as a proxy for the model’s predictive limit. PACE
instead selects replanning boundaries from prominent low-speed valleys in the kinematic profile of a
single decoded action chunk, without requiring model-internal signals or additional chunk samples.

4 Simulation experiments
Experimental setup.We evaluate PACE on 50 RoboTwin2.0 bimanual manipulation tasks, training
one π0.5 checkpoint per task under the standard protocol. All methods share the same checkpoint
and differ only in test-time execution. Unless otherwise noted, ourfull evaluation protocoluses 900
episodes per task (three seeds, each with three batches of 100 episodes), with success rates averaged
over all 50 tasks. Task-level tables show six representative tasks balanced across short-, medium-,
and long-duration groups. Full results and training details are in Appendix B.
Overview of experiments.We compare PACE as a default execution strategy with fixed-horizon
and adaptive baselines (Sec. 4.1) and analyze its horizon adaptation within rollouts (Sec. 4.2). We
ablate the phase profile, training prediction horizon, and threshold sharing (Sec. 4.3), and use
task-specific fixed-horizon sweeps as a diagnostic upper bound (Sec. 4.4).
4.1 Simulation results
We compare PACE against fixed-horizon execution withH∈ {5,25,50} , corresponding to short,
intermediate, and full-chunk prefixes (recall Htrain=50). We also evaluate Adaptive Action Chunking
(AAC) [21] and AutoHorizon [22] using the same task-specific π0.5 checkpoints. Our bimanual joint-
space adaptation of AAC estimates action entropy from 20 sampled chunks per policy query. PACE
calibrates one threshold per task from the checkpoint’s existing training demonstrations, without
using evaluation rollouts, success labels, or a fixed-horizon search (Appendix A.3).
Table 1 shows that PACE achieves the highest 50-task average, exceeding the best global fixed horizon
(H= 25 ) by 6.4 points and AAC and AutoHorizon by approximately 5.1 and 5.2 points, respectively.
PACE also exceeds the strongest fixed horizon on every displayed task and achieves the highest mean
on five of the six. AAC leads on Pick Bottles (48.9% versus 48.2%).

4.2 Visualizing adaptive execution horizons
We visualize a representativeplace_shoe rollout in Fig. 3 to examine how PACE adapts its execution
horizon. Selected horizons range from 14 to 37 steps, with longer horizons covering coherent motion
(the approach in Plan 3, 37 steps) and shorter horizons near low-speed transitions (Plan 2, 14 steps).
A fixed horizon cannot adapt to this within-episode structure.
The discarded suffixes show how PACE limits open-loop execution as the scene changes. PACE
replans at boundaries selected from the predicted motion structure, and the new plan often differs
from the discarded suffix over the overlapping interval, as seen in the mismatched solid and dashed
curves of adjacent plans around t=83. This limits reliance on stale observations while preserving
long horizons for coherent motion, helping explain the gains over fixed-horizon baselines in Sec. 4.1.

4.3 Ablation analysis
We examine how motion-profile construction, the training prediction horizon, and threshold sharing
affect PACE’s performance. We evaluate motion-profile variants and compare shared versus per-task
thresholds on all 50 RoboTwin2.0 tasks, with unchanged policy checkpoints. To study the training
prediction horizon Htrain, we train policies to predict action chunks of different lengths. We compare
their success rates at the same fixed execution horizon to assess whether supervision over longer
action sequences improves the quality of the executed prefix. Since each setting requires retraining,
we evaluate them on six representative tasks. Further details and results appear in Appendix C.
Phase-profile construction.We compare joint-space and Cartesian speed profiles, with and without
smoothing, to examine whether PACE depends on a specific motion representation and how smoothing
affects boundary selection. The Cartesian variant computes end-effector linear speed through forward
kinematics. We keep the valley-selection rule and calibration percentile fixed across all four variants.
Table 2 (left) shows that smoothing improves success in both spaces and increases the mean selected
execution horizon. This pattern suggests that smoothing suppresses noisy local valleys that would
otherwise trigger overly frequent replanning. After smoothing, Cartesian and joint-space profiles
achieve comparable success, while the joint-space variant selects longer execution horizons on
average. We adopt the smoothed joint-space profile by default. It is within 0.3 points of the best
variant, matches the policy action space, and avoids forward kinematics at every replanning query.
Training prediction horizon.Each row of Table 2 (right) fixes Heval and varies Htrain; pairs
with Htrain < H eval are infeasible because the policy predicts too few actions. Success increases
monotonically with Htrain, and the gain of Htrain=50 over the shortest feasible target shrinks from
+20.7 at Heval=10 to +3.3 at Heval=40. This trend shows that longer training targets provide
stronger sequence-level supervision: they constrain not just the action after the observation, but how
motion evolves across a longer expert segment. Since the executed prefix is predicted jointly with the
rest of the chunk, it inherits this structure. We adoptH train=50as the default prediction horizon.

5 Real-robot experiments
We evaluate PACE on real robots in two settings: RoboChallenge [ 25] and our in-lab
place_object_on_plate task family. RoboChallenge is a public real-robot benchmark; we
evaluate stack_bowls and put_pen_into_pencil_case with 30 trials per method per task. In
place_object_on_plate, a Franka robot places one of five objects onto a plate in our laboratory
setup. Within each task, all methods use the same fine-tuned π0.5 checkpoint and differ only in the
test-time execution scheme. We report task Score and full-completion rate Succ. for both settings;
task definitions, scoring rules, training details, and per-object in-lab results appear in Appendix E.
5.1 Real-robot results
Table 5 compares PACE with the full-chunk execution baseline (H= 50 ) on the three real-robot
tasks. PACE improves both task score and success rate on every task, raising the average success rate
from 50.7% to 70.4% and the average task score from 60.7 to 77.7, with each task weighted equally.
To assess whether more frequent replanning with a fixed horizon yields similar performance, we
further evaluate H= 5 and H= 25 on the Franka task. PACE achieves an 88% success rate,
compared with 87% for H= 5 and 82% for H= 25 . It thus attains a success rate comparable to
H= 5while requiring 84.6% fewer policy queries per successful episode (Appendix E.3).
5.2 PACE horizon selection in a successful rollout
Figure 5 visualizes a successful stack_bowls rollout and shows that PACE adjusts its execution
horizon according to the local manipulation phase rather than a fixed replanning cadence. During
approach and transport, the method selects long horizons (27, 30, 31 actions), allowing the robot
to maintain continuous motion while open-loop execution remains reliable. Near contact-sensitive
alignment, where small pose errors determine whether the bowl stacks, the horizon contracts to 7
actions so the next query can incorporate an updated observation. Once alignment is resolved, the
horizon expands again to 43, showing that the method does not remain in a high-frequency replanning
mode when a coherent motion segment becomes available.
This behavior mirrors the simulation analysis in Sec. 4.2 and helps explain the real-robot gains
in Table 5: PACE introduces feedback where stale observations are most costly, while avoiding
unnecessary queries during smooth motion. Additional rollout visualizations for representative
RoboTwin2.0 and RoboChallenge tasks are provided in Appendix F.

5.3 Failure case and scope of test-time execution control
Figure 6 shows a failed put_pen_into_pencil_case rollout. The task is strictly sequential: the
left gripper must fully open the pencil case before the right arm inserts the pen. In this rollout, PACE
selects short horizons (7 and 6 actions) around the insertion phase, so the policy refreshes its action
chunk multiple times from updated observations. Yet the pencil case remains only partially opened
throughout the segment, and the task fails.
The failure is therefore not due to feedback timing, but to the learned action distribution of the base
policy. PACE already shortens the execution horizon near insertion and refreshes the chunk from
updated observations, but the refreshed chunks are still produced by the same policy and do not yield
task-completing behavior in this rollout. This case highlights the boundary of test-time execution
control: it can decide when to query the policy, but it cannot compensate when the policy itself fails
to generate actions sufficient for task completion.
6 Limitations
PACE cannot resolve failures when the base policy continues to predict incomplete or incorrect
actions (Sec. 5.3). Its replanning rule can miss phase transitions without clear low-speed valleys. If
no valley is accepted, PACE falls back to the maximum execution horizon. Effectiveness with policy
families and action representations beyond those evaluated here remains to be assessed. Threshold
calibration requires training demonstrations, either per task or pooled across tasks for a shared
threshold. Sharing avoids separate per-task calibration but retains this data requirement. Earlier
replanning can increase policy queries relative to full-chunk execution, raising inference cost even
though the selector is inexpensive.
7 Conclusion
We showed that the execution horizon is a critical deployment variable with strongly task-dependent
and non-monotonic effects on success. PACE selects execution horizons online from the phase-
dependent kinematics of predicted action chunks, using prominent low-speed valleys as candidate
replanning boundaries without additional training. On RoboTwin2.0, PACE improves the average
success rate of π0.5 over both fixed-horizon and adaptive execution baselines. Applying PACE to
GR00T N1.7 on LIBERO also yields the highest overall success rate among the evaluated strategies,
supporting its applicability across different base policies, benchmarks, and action representations.
Real-robot evaluations further demonstrate improvements in task score and success rate over full-
chunk execution. Ablations support motion-profile smoothing and longer training prediction horizons,
while a shared threshold retains most gains. Gains over matched fixed-horizon baselines further
support adaptation within a rollout. PACE offers a practical way to improve task performance through
adaptive execution without modifying or retraining the policy.

B.1.3 Adaptive baseline implementations
Shared experimental setting.AutoHorizon and AAC use the same task-specific π0.5 checkpoints
as the fixed-horizon baselines and PACE, without additional policy training. Both use ten flow-
sampling steps to predict 50-step chunks of decoded 14-D actions, with six absolute joint-position
commands and one gripper command per arm. They follow the evaluation protocol in Appendix B.1.1.
For each baseline, the three batches within a task and evaluation seed reuse the same set of 100
evaluation scenes. Their selector settings are fixed across tasks and are not tuned using evaluation
success labels.
AutoHorizon.We implement AutoHorizon [ 22] in JAX following the official implementation.
The selector uses model-internal action-token self-attention from denoising step 3 and applies the
bidirectional soft-pointer rule with τ= 0.3 , entropy quantile 0.9, and run length 1. The robot
executes the selected prefix of the decoded action chunk, discards the remaining actions, and queries
the policy again using a new observation.
AAC adapted to bimanual joint-space actions.We adapt AAC [ 21] to RoboTwin’s bimanual
joint-space actions and sample 20 candidate chunks per policy query. At each prediction step, we
estimate Gaussian entropy from the six normalized joint commands of each arm across the candidates,
with covariance regularization 10−6I. For each gripper, we binarize its decoded commands at 0.5
and compute Bernoulli entropy.
Movement is measured from the decoded absolute joint targets relative to the current state, summing
the arm-wise displacements and weighting gripper toggles by 0.2. We use the movement threshold
of 3.0 reported in the AAC supplementary material. The final execution horizon is the larger of the
horizon selected by AAC’s prefix-entropy turning-point rule and the shortest prefix satisfying the
movement constraint.
To generate AAC’s 20 candidate chunks, ourπ0.5 implementation encodes the image–language–state
prefix once per query and reuses its KV cache across candidates. The action head processes candidate
0 separately and the remaining 19 candidates as a batch. This grouping preserves candidate 0’s
agreement with standardπ 0.5 sampling. The robot executes the selected prefix of candidate 0.

speed-profile construction and 5th-percentile rule. The resulting threshold is used unchanged across
all 50 tasks. All compared execution strategies use the same task-specific π0.5 checkpoints, and all
other PACE settings and evaluation conditions remain unchanged.

rate over all 50 tasks for each of three evaluation seeds. Table 3 reports the mean and sample standard
deviation of these three averages.

approximately 1.8 points over the shared threshold, with a 95% confidence interval of[+1.18,+2.35] .
We compute each interval with a paired bootstrap over the 50 task-level differences using unrounded

D.3 Phase alignment
To examine whether PACE replans near motion-phase transitions, we compare its executed valley-
triggered boundaries with independently annotated transitions. The analysis covers 89 successful
rollouts from three RoboTwin2.0 tasks: Place A2B Right, Place Phone Stand, and Pick Dual Bottles.
Blind annotation and matching.Annotators identified motion-phase transitions from paired head-
camera and external-observer videos without access to PACE’s replanning boundaries or boundary
overlays. After the annotations were finalized, we extracted the executed valley-triggered boundaries
from the rollout logs. We matched boundaries one-to-one with annotated transitions within ±5
control steps (±0.1 s at 50 Hz). We chose the matching with the largest number of pairs, breaking
ties by the smallest total temporal distance. The one-to-one constraint prevents multiple boundaries
from being matched to the same transition.
Of the 391 executed replanning boundaries, six were Hmax fallbacks and were counted separately.
The primary alignment metric uses the remaining 385 valley-triggered boundaries.
Metrics and temporal controls.Alignment precision is the fraction of executed valley-triggered
boundaries matched to annotated transitions. Transition coverage is the fraction of annotated transi-
tions matched to a boundary. For each task, we obtain 95% confidence intervals for PACE precision
by bootstrapping the audited successful rollouts at the trajectory level.
To assess whether the observed alignment exceeds chance levels, we compare PACE with two offline
controls that retain the same number of boundaries. The first samples boundary times uniformly at
random with a minimum spacing of 10 control steps. The second circularly shifts PACE’s boundaries
while preserving their relative spacing. Each control is evaluated over 10,000 randomizations.

E.2 Fine-tuning configuration
For each RoboChallenge task, we fine-tune a task-specific π0.5 policy initialized from the PI0.5 base
checkpoint. Training is performed on four H200 GPUs with FSDP and bfloat16 precision. We use
AdamW with a global batch size of 256, global gradient clipping at norm 1.0, EMA decay 0.99, and
eight data-loader workers. The learning rate follows a cosine decay schedule with 1000 warmup
steps, peak learning rate 2.5×10 −5, decay horizon 30,000 steps, and final learning rate 2.5×10 −6.
For all reported RoboChallenge trials, we deploy the30,000-step checkpoint.
For place_object_on_plate, we fine-tune a single π0.5 checkpoint over all five object variants
using 301 demonstrations. The training recipe follows the RoboChallenge setting except that we use
a batch size of 32 and train on one RTX A6000 Pro GPU. We also deploy the30,000-step checkpoint
for all in-lab trials.
