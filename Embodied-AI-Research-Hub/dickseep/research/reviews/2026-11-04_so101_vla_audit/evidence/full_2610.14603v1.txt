Abstract
Due to the high cost of hardware experiments, robot policies are predominantly evaluated by comparing their empirical performance on a limited number of hardware trials. However, finite-sample statistical uncertainty can lead to erroneous conclusions, especially when ranking multiple policies using a fixed batch of evaluation data. We propose AnyRank: a sequential method for multi-policy ranking that (i) adaptively allocates trials to hard-to-separate policies, (ii) provides (partial) rankings at any stopping time while bounding the probability of an incorrect ranking, and (iii) optionally exploits prior information to prioritize hardware evaluations more efficiently. This prior information could be informed from expert intuition, a small set of held-out hardware evaluations, or from policy rollouts in a simulator or an action-conditioned video world model. Notably, the prior can be imperfect; while a strong prior can provide efficiency gains on hardware, any inaccuracies in prior policy rankings do not invalidate the statistical assurances of AnyRank. We achieve the aforementioned properties through novel theoretical contributions that combine sequential testing with multiple-hypothesis graphical testing approaches. Experiments in simulation and real-world policy evaluation benchmarks show that AnyRank requires up to 50% fewer hardware evaluations compared to existing statistically rigorous approaches, while guarding against the incorrect rankings produced by heuristic methods used by state-of-the-art benchmarks.

1 Introduction
Robot learning faces an evaluation challenge. As policies become increasingly capable of generalizing to a broad range of tasks, environments, and embodiments, rigorous evaluation and benchmarking become critical bottlenecks [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. In particular, the analytical opacity of state-of-the-art (SOTA) policies places a significant burden on empirical evaluation.
How can we identify the most critical design choices for our policy architecture, data mixture, or training algorithm? Can we confidently conclude if improved performance resulted from a particular intervention rather than another? How can we determine if our proposed method is genuinely better than baselines? Such questions demand reliable policy ranking methods using empirical measurements of performance.
In contrast to domains such as computer vision and natural language processing, evaluation in robotics is fundamentally limited by the time- and labor-intensive nature of hardware experiments. As a result, policy performance is usually summarized with a point estimate (e.g., empirical mean) of task-relevant performance metrics computed over a small number (20−3020-30) of hardware rollouts [1]. However, comparing policies in this way is inherently unreliable due to finite-sample statistical variations in tasks, environments, embodiments, and policy stochasticity. For instance, the ranking of two closely competing policies can be easily flipped if one policy achieves a few more successful rollouts than the other by chance. This risk of incorrect inferences becomes particularly pronounced when a large number of policies must be ranked using a fixed batch of evaluation data.

Commonly used approaches [7] for producing confidence intervals or p-values often make asymptotic assumptions that are not valid for a finite number of samples (see Section 2 for examples). Moreover, comparisons based on confidence intervals tend to have less statistical power than direct hypothesis testing [8], leading to highly indeterminate rankings with many ties. On the other hand, preference-based ranking methods such as Bradley-Terry models [9] tend to result in definitive rankings, but can have high error rates in the non-asymptotic regime, as we show in our experiments. While simulators and world models offer promising pathways to scalable evaluation [10, 11, 3, 12, 13, 14], the resulting inferences do not necessarily reflect real-world performance. As such, estimates from simulation can serve as imperfect priors that must be combined with gold-standard hardware trials for reliable policy ranking [15].
We posit that an ideal framework for policy ranking in robotics should satisfy three primary desiderata. First, it should produce statistically reliable conclusions even with the small numbers of experimental trials that are common in robotics. Specifically, we must bound the probability of an incorrect ranking to any given practitioner-specified value. The resulting ranking should be anytime-valid by producing a (possibly partial) ranking at any decision time, while allowing for the possibility of continued evaluations without the risk of p-hacking [16]. Second, it should be sample efficient by adaptively and actively allocating trials to hard-to-separate policies. Finally, an ideal policy ranking method should allow for the incorporation of priors (e.g., from researcher intuition from preliminary experiments in simulation, held-out hardware evaluations, or rollouts in a world model) to improve sample efficiency while guarding against imperfectly specified priors.
Statement of Contributions.
We propose AnyRank, a framework for policy ranking that satisfies the properties highlighted above: statistical reliability, anytime validity, sample efficiency, and safe incorporation of priors. In order to achieve this, we formulate policy ranking as a multiple-hypothesis testing problem where data is collected sequentially. Our key theoretical contribution is a novel combination of safe anytime-valid inference [17] with graphical procedures for multiple-hypothesis testing [18]. The resulting algorithm actively allocates evaluations to policies that are more difficult to distinguish statistically, adaptively assigns the global confidence budget to different hypotheses while bounding the probability of incorrect rankings (i.e., family-wise error rate control [19]), and initializes hyperparameters in the testing procedure to leverage prior information. Extensive experiments on real-world policy evaluation benchmarks such as RoboArena [2] and the TRI-LBM dataset [5] demonstrate that our method requires up to 50% fewer hardware evaluations compared to existing statistically rigorous approaches, while guarding against the incorrect rankings produced by imperfect priors or heuristic methods used by state-of-the-art benchmarks.
2 Related Work
Heuristic Approaches. Real-world experiments are the gold-standard for evaluating generalist robot policies, but are not scalable. Thus, robot policy evaluation on hardware is usually limited to small sample sizes, often yielding statistically inconclusive results [20, 1, 5]. To evaluate generalist policies in a wide range of environments, the robotics community has invested in distributing evaluation effort across a network of evaluators [2] using Bradley-Terry approaches [9] to rank policies based on human preferences, but offers no statistical guarantees of a correct ranking and can have high rates of error in practice (Section 5). A scalable alternative to hardware is policy evaluation in simulation, where physics-based simulators have traditionally been used to benchmark policy performance [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 14, 3, 26, 27]. More recently, action-conditioned video world models [28, 29, 12, 11, 30, 31, 10] have emerged as a potential solution due to fast scene instantiation and the ability to simulate deformable objects. While these methods often show empirical correlation between simulated and hardware evaluations, they do not provide statistical assurances, making it challenging to infer relative policy rankings under novel tasks or scenes. In contrast, our approach provides policy rankings that are finite-sample valid, and can use simulation evaluations as a prior to inform policy evaluations on hardware.
Sequential Hypothesis Testing.
Our work builds upon ideas from the Neyman-Pearson statistical testing framework [32]; for policy comparison, this framework has primarily been applied in fixed-sample-size contexts (i.e., batch evaluation methods) [33, 7, 34]. Allowing the evaluator to adaptively stop trials (termed optional stopping [35]) can invalidate statistical assurances by inflating the Type-I error (i.e., false positive) rate unless appropriate corrections are applied [17]; this phenomenon underlies many common forms of p-hacking [8]. Further, the robotics literature typically relies on only asymptotically valid statistical procedures, even though hardware evaluation budgets are often too small for such approximations to be reliable [36, 37, 38, 39, 40].
Sequential [35], nonasymptotic approaches [41, 42, 43] allow the evaluator to choose when to stop evaluations while ensuring control of Type-I error. This data adaptivity promises significant and interpretable improvement in sample efficiency via allowing feedback from the current evaluation evidence [44, 45, 46, 47]. Recent work in safe anytime-valid inference (SAVI) [48, 49, 17, 50] constructs robust families of stochastic processes that implicitly account for optional stopping biases in general, nonparametric settings, allowing the evaluator to adapt online to save evaluation effort under general progress metrics. Building on SAVI methods, efficient, sequential and non-asymptotic pairwise robot policy comparison methods have been developed for general progress metrics [51].
Rigorous Robot Policy Evaluation.
Rigorous robot policy evaluation has emphasized constructing confidence intervals to estimate the mean performance of a single policy [15, 52, 53], or comparing binary pairs of policies [20, 51]. Ranking multiple policies, however, is a composite, multi-hypothesis testing problem which prior work only considers via inefficient Bonferroni (i.e., union bound) corrections [53, 20, 51, 5, 54]. Such corrections can significantly limit statistical power because the number of pairwise hypotheses scales quadratically in the number of policies. By contrast, our work addresses this limitation via graphical hypothesis testing approaches [18, 55] to safely re-allocate the evaluation budget to pairwise comparisons that are harder to distinguish, improving power. While graphical procedures [18] are state-of-the-art for batch testing, they do not directly extend to sequential evaluation settings. Thus, our work also differs from this literature by extension of these methods to adaptive sampling and sequential evaluation settings. Both contributions are necessary to ensure efficiency gains over classical Bonferroni corrections and valid policy rankings.
3 Problem Formulation: Policy Ranking as Hypothesis Testing
Given a set of MM robot policies, our goal is to rank the policies according to their mean performance on a (complicated and unknown) real-world task and environment distribution 𝒟\mathcal{D}. We assume that policy evaluations are carried out on i.i.d. samples e∼𝒟e\sim\mathcal{D} of tasks and environments. Let RR be a shared, real-valued and bounded metric (as in [51]) of task performance (e.g., binary success or partial progress score). We denote by rj​(e)r_{j}(e) the performance of policy πj\pi_{j} on environment ee. We let μj:=𝔼e∼𝒟​[rj​(e)]\mu_{j}:=\mathbb{E}_{e\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_{j}(e)] denote the (unknown) true expected value of the performance metric for πj\pi_{j}. Thus, policy ranking refers to reliably ordering {μj}j=1M\{\mu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M} using a small number of evaluation rollouts.
Pairwise Policy Comparison.
Consider a pair of robot policies πj,πj′\pi_{j},\pi_{j^{\prime}} with (unknown) true means μj,μj′\mu_{j},\mu_{j^{\prime}}. To rank them, we require two one-sided hypothesis tests:
(1) ℋ0j,j′ (null): 𝔼e∼𝒟[rj(e)]≥𝔼e∼𝒟[rj′(e)], ℋ1j,j′ (alternative): 𝔼e∼𝒟[rj(e)]<𝔼e∼𝒟[rj′(e)];(2) ℋ0j′,j (null): 𝔼e∼𝒟[rj′(e)]≥𝔼e∼𝒟[rj(e)], ℋ1j′,j (alternative): 𝔼e∼𝒟[rj′(e)]<𝔼e∼𝒟[rj(e)].\begin{split}\text{(1) }\mathcal{H}^{0}_{j,j^{\prime}}\text{ (null): }&\mathbb{E}_{e\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_{j}(e)]\geq\mathbb{E}_{e\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_{j^{\prime}}(e)]\text{, }\mathcal{H}^{1}_{j,j^{\prime}}\text{ (alternative): }\mathbb{E}_{e\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_{j}(e)]<\mathbb{E}_{e\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_{j^{\prime}}(e)];\\
\text{(2) }\mathcal{H}^{0}_{j^{\prime},j}\text{ (null): }&\mathbb{E}_{e\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_{j^{\prime}}(e)]\geq\mathbb{E}_{e\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_{j}(e)]\text{, }\mathcal{H}^{1}_{j^{\prime},j}\text{ (alternative): }\mathbb{E}_{e\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_{j^{\prime}}(e)]<\mathbb{E}_{e\sim\mathcal{D}}[r_{j}(e)].\end{split}
If test (1) rejects its null hypothesis, then we can conclude that μj<μj′\mu_{j}<\mu_{j^{\prime}}, and vice-versa if test (2) rejects. Due to the statistical uncertainty in a limited number of hardware rollouts, the probability of incorrect inference from hypothesis testing should be bounded with a pre-specified risk level α∈(0,1)\alpha\in(0,1). Concretely, if ℋj,j′0\mathcal{H}^{0}_{j,j^{\prime}} is indeed true, then a test procedure controls the Type-1 error at rate α\alpha if the probability of the test falsely concluding that ℋj,j′1\mathcal{H}^{1}_{j,j^{\prime}} is true is bounded by α\alpha. Such false conclusions are termed Type-I errors, and a testing procedure that provides such assurances is called a Type-I error-controlling procedure. For a two-sided test at level α\alpha, we run both one-sided tests each at budget α\alpha, as it is highly unlikely for two policies to have identical mean performance over complex distribution of tasks and environments (see Remark 3).
Multiple Policy Comparison. For multi-policy ranking, a sufficient condition is to consistently decide K=(M2)K=\binom{M}{2} pairwise comparisons, yielding a multi-hypothesis testing problem ℍ\mathbb{H}. A ranking is complete if all policy pairs are correctly decided, and partial if a fraction of pairs are correctly ranked. The probability of a test incorrectly deciding at least one pair is the family-wise error rate (FWER). Standard approaches to control FWER split the risk budget between all pairwise comparisons [5], which substantially reduces statistical power because the number of pairwise tests grows quadratically in MM, and hypotheses with smaller risk budgets require more hardware evaluations.
Problem Statement. Given MM robot policies and risk level α∗\alpha^{*}, the goal is to rank them in as few evaluations as possible while maintaining high statistical power and FWER control. We seek a sampling and stopping test procedure S∗S^{*} that balances expected sample complexity 𝔼​[T]​(S)\mathbb{E}[T](S) and expected statistical power (1−𝔼​[F]​(S)1-\mathbb{E}[F](S)) while maintaining FWER control at α∗\alpha^{*}:

where 𝔼​[F]​(S)\mathbb{E}[F](S) is the expected fraction of pairwise hypotheses that the test procedure SS will fail to reject. While Equation 1 is difficult to solve in general, our goal is to design a practical evaluation framework that approaches the optimal tradeoff while strictly controlling FWER. This requires an active evaluation strategy: different pairwise hypotheses may require different amounts of evidence to reach a decision, and choosing which policy to evaluate next is important for efficient policy ranking.
4 Method: Multi-Policy Ranking via Sequential Graphical Testing
We propose AnyRank as a statistically rigorous policy ranking procedure that decomposes the ranking problem into two fundamental components. First, the problem of multi-policy comparison is reduced to a structured set of pairwise policy comparison decisions using graphical procedures for principled multi-hypothesis correction. Our first novel contribution is providing a general framework to incorporate prior knowledge (e.g., from simulation) in the graph structure to accelerate policy ranking. Next, evaluation of pairwise comparisons is addressed using the sequential, nonparametric evaluation procedures of Snyder et al. [51]. To combine these proceduresrequires carefully reconciling batch graphical procedures with sequential evaluation methods to ensure FWER control. Proving that AnyRank indeed maintains FWER control is our second main technical contribution.
Graphical Methods.
Graphical testing procedures [18] are state-of-the-art methods for batch multiple hypothesis testing. Each hypothesis is initially assigned a fraction of the global risk budget α∗\alpha^{*}, which is reallocated to other hypotheses upon rejection. Formally, a graphical test specifies an initial allocation {α0[i]}\{\alpha_{0}^{[i]}\} of the Type-I error budget that sums to α∗\alpha^{*}. This is represented by weights {w[i]≥0:∑iw[i]=1}\{w^{[i]}\geq 0:\sum_{i}w^{[i]}=1\} where the initial allocation is α0[i]=w[i]​α∗\alpha_{0}^{[i]}=w^{[i]}\alpha^{*}. Second, it specifies the reallocation structure {w[j​k]≥0:j,k∈{1,…,K}}\{w^{[jk]}\geq 0:j,k\in\{1,...,K\}\}, such that when any hypothesis ℋj\mathcal{H}_{j} is rejected, the budget assigned to hypothesis ℋj\mathcal{H}_{j} is recycled to increase the error budgets of the remaining hypotheses. Formally, when hypothesis ℋj\mathcal{H}_{j} is rejected, the reallocation weight w[j​k]w^{[jk]} denotes the fraction of α[j]\alpha^{[j]} that is added (i.e., ‘recycled’ or re-allocated) to α[k]\alpha^{[k]} : α[k]←α[k]+w[j​k]​α[j]\alpha^{[k]}\leftarrow\alpha^{[k]}+w^{[jk]}\alpha^{[j]}. Here, w[j​j]=0w^{[jj]}=0, and a stochastic structure is maintained: ∑kw[j​k]=1,∀j∈{1,…,K}\sum_{k}w^{[jk]}=1,\penalty\ \forall j\in\{1,\dots,K\}. Prior Knowledge. We incorporate prior estimates of policy performance differences into both the initial risk allocation and the graph weights for the multitest hypothesis graph (see Section A.2). Specifically, a softmax-style allocation assigns larger risk budgets to policy pairs predicted to be easier to distinguish, enabling faster decisions with fewer hardware trials and earlier risk reallocation to more difficult hypotheses. We also consider two ablations of the graphical structure, controlled by the softmax inverse temperature parameter γ\gamma: γ=0\gamma=0 yields a uniform risk allocation that ignores prior information, while γ=∞\gamma=\infty yields a chain graph that uses the prior to order hypotheses from easiest to hardest to distinguish. In the batch setting, these correspond to the special cases of Holm’s procedure and fixed-sequence testing, respectively (see Section A.1 for details).

Robust, General, and Sequential Pairwise Comparison.
While traditional policy comparison is conducted in a batch setting, recent work [20, 51] introduces a sequential approach to pairwise policy comparison that controls for Type-I error at any stopping time. This offers two key advantages: i) if the difference in μj\mu_{j} and μj′\mu_{j}^{\prime} is large, the evaluator can stop early after accumulating sufficient evidence for the test to decide the better policy, and ii) if the policies are close in performance, the test remains inconclusive, giving the evaluator the option to collect more evaluations without invalidating statistical assurances.
Snyder et al. [51] recently presented a SOTA sequential method (N-SCORE) for general two-policy comparison under bounded performance measures that guarantees Type-I error control at a tunable level α\alpha.
N-SCORE is a safe anytime-valid inference (SAVI) procedure [17] for policy comparison; as such, it maintains a canonical p-process (pt)t≥0(p_{t})_{t\geq 0} ([49], Proposition 12.1): a sequence of p-values that gives a valid Type-I error upper bound at all possible stopping times. This p-process allows the practitioner to ‘peek’ at the intermediate evaluation results for the atomic comparison and wait just until pt<αip_{t}<\alpha_{i} is achieved before stopping the comparison – this is the robust feedback mechanism which will enable AnyRank to utilize efficient active evaluation online. Procedurally, for each pairwise comparison {ℋi}i=1K\{\mathcal{H}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K} we will instantiate an N-SCORE test which will, from any finite dataset of tt evaluations, induce a set of anytime p-values {pi,t}i=1K\{p_{i,t}\}_{i=1}^{K} corresponding to the evidence against each atomic null hypothesis.
Efficient Active Sampling.
Standard sequential policy comparison approaches assume equal amounts of data for all policies [20, 51]. However, this is not required, and an active (i.e., feedback) selection rule in fact allows for significant hardware savings without invalidating the statistical assurances required for AnyRank. This active evaluation sampling rule, denoted SS, defines the selection of the next policy to evaluate and also encodes the stopping mechanism of the evaluation. Importantly, because anytime-valid p-values (generated by the pairwise subroutine N-SCORE) are robust to measurable stopping times, the evaluation state of the underlying pairwise comparisons and the structure of the graph instantiation may be explicitly encoded in the policy selection mechanism. For AnyRank, we design the selection mechanism around the notion of advantage, which is understood as a proxy for the value of sampling each policy with greater frequency than uniformly at random. Concretely, policies are ‘advantaged’ when they are associated with comparisons possessing high α[i]\alpha^{[i]}, as these comparisons are in general the most efficient to reject.

Sequential Graphical Testing.
Combining sequential and data-adaptive policy selection for evaluation and batch-only graphical procedures for multihypothesis correction is challenging because the latter is generally incompatible with data-adaptive, sequential procedures. Without careful correction, the statistical assurances that allow the risk ‘recycling’ are invalidated.
However, the robustness of anytime-valid p-values to arbitrary stopping times reconciles the conflict. In particular, if a hypothesis ℋi\mathcal{H}_{i} rejects at some total sample complexity tt, we can simulate the standard graphical update procedure [18]:

Since N-SCORE [51] provides anytime-valid p-values under adaptive sampling and stopping, their validity is preserved even when stopping decisions depend on simulated graph updates. Crucially, the final graphical testing procedure requires only valid p-values, as guaranteed by N-SCORE. Note, however, initial graph structure (i.e., initial risk assignment and reallocation strategy via graph weights) is fixed prior to collecting any evaluations, and cannot be modified after the evaluation period starts. Thus, by construction, AnyRank (Algorithm 1) guarantees FWER control, as formalized in Theorem 1. We provide further validation of FWER control on synthetic data in Appendix F.
Theorem 1 (Validity of AnyRank).
Given a risk budget α∗∈(0,1)\alpha^{*}\in(0,1), construct a valid graphical test GG over the set of pairwise hypotheses, and a measureable sampling rule S⁡(ℱ,G,Nm​a​x,α∗)S(\mathcal{F},G;N_{max},\alpha^{*}) which encodes finite optional stopping of the evaluation. Then, the probability that the evaluation procedure in Algorithm 1 makes one or more incorrect ranking decisions is upper bounded by α∗\alpha^{*}.
Remark 1 (Transitive closure).
If we have resolved hypotheses ℋi\mathcal{H}_{i} and ℋj\mathcal{H}_{j}, respectively concluding that πA<πB\pi_{A}<\pi_{B} and πB<πC\pi_{B}<\pi_{C}, we can by transitivity also reject hypothesis ℋk\mathcal{H}_{k} that compares πA\pi_{A} and πC\pi_{C} to conclude that πA<πC\pi_{A}<\pi_{C} without further evaluation, even if pkp_{k} is not in a rejection range based on its N-SCORE subroutine. Evaluation with a very poor prior is one instance where this might arise in practice. We formalize this step in Corollary 1, with the proof of validity deferred to Appendix B.
Corollary 1.
Consider the AnyRank procedure defined in Algorithm 1, modified such that the final ranking decisions are augmented with a transitive closure filter:
Dt←TransitiveClosure⁡(Graphical⁡({pt,i}i=1K,G,α∗)).D_{t}\leftarrow\mathrm{TransitiveClosure}(\mathrm{Graphical}(\{p_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{K},G,\alpha^{*})).
Then, if the base instantiation controls FWER at level α∗\alpha^{*}, the transitively closed (and, as a result, more powerful) instantiation also controls FWER at level α∗\alpha^{*}.
5 Experiments
We investigate the following research questions:
R1.
Statistical Reliability. Does the AnyRank family maintain statistical reliability (i.e., FWER control), even when specified with incorrect simulation priors? Moreover, does this statistical reliability guard against policy ranking failure modes observed in Bradley-Terry baselines?
R2.
Efficiency. Does AnyRank increase statistical power by improving on both the number of hypotheses rejected and total sample complexity compared to other FWER-controlling methods?
R3.
Impact of Priors. Does the sample complexity of AnyRank improve with better priors?
R4.
Scalability. How does the sample complexity of AnyRank scale with the number of policies?
Experiment Setup. We consider three sets of hardware policy evaluations: i) RoboArena evaluations comprising crowd-sourced evaluations for generalist policies in diverse environments and tasks [2], ii) Large Behavior Models (LBM) evaluations [5], and iii) task-specific hardware policy evaluations on DROID setup (termed as DROID-task). In total, we consider over 7000 policy rollouts across 20+ policies. We use both world model rollouts and heldout evaluations as priors for AnyRank, AnyRank∞{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{\infty}, and weighted Bonferroni methods. Additionally, we use synthetic data to study scalability of AnyRank, along with additional analysis on FWER control in Appendix F. Unless otherwise noted, we use the global confidence level α∗=0.1\alpha^{*}=0.1.

Baselines.
Bradley-Terry is a probabilistic modeling approach that scores policies based on pairwise comparison of preferences using maximum likelihood estimation [56], and policies are then ranked on the estimated scores. In addition, RoboArena presents three variants which we compare against: i) Bradley-Terry (Davidson) which accommodates ties with an additional scalar parameter, ii) Bradley-Terry (EM) which extends the classical model by adding task effect parameters, and iii) Bradley-Terry (EM w/ partial progress) which
uses partial task progress scores in addition to pairwise preferences. None of these methods guarantee FWER control. Additionally, we compare against sequentialized uniform and weighted Bonferroni corrections, which control FWER by distributing the total risk budget across individual hypothesis tests and applying a union bound. In the weighted Bonferroni baseline, the initial risk allocation is made in proportion to the difficulty of comparison informed by the prior; policies that have closer prior performances are given a larger fraction of the budget.
Metrics. An optimal multi-policy ranking procedure will return a correct and complete ranking in as few trials as possible. First, we check whether a ranking procedure maintains FWER Control, which requires that rankings to be correct at any stopping time with high probability. Failed FWER control manifests in two key ways: i) miscalibration, where the procedure returns a ranking decision before sufficient evidence has accumulated, and ii) inconsistency, where the ranking procedure changes the ordering of at least two policies as more data is collected. Second, for methods that control FWER, we measure statistical power with the number of pairwise hypotheses rejected and sample complexity.

Reliability of Policy Rankings (R1).
We focus our analysis of ranking reliability on RoboArena, which provides the largest set of policies and hardware evaluations, along with pairwise human preferences. Table 1 lists Bradley-Terry policy rankings on RoboArena, along with rankings produced by FWER-controlling methods limited to the same number of total trials as Bradley--Terry methods33

Each RoboArena scene can have multiple policy evaluations, but only has one set of pairwise preferences. Unlike Bradley–Terry methods that always produce a complete ranking, FWER-controlling methods return partial rankings if one or more hypotheses cannot be rejected (see Table 1). This is often due to the number of evaluations being insufficient in relation to the difference between the true means of the policies being considered. AnyRank (and its ablations) are tunably robust and temporally consistent: they will not mislead the evaluator when the evaluation dataset is small or when there are many policies present, and will not switch ordering as the amount of data increases. Figure 3 visualizes AnyRank’s policy rankings for RoboArena-7 using violin plots to depict the empirical spread of raw evaluation data and compact letters [57] to denote ranking assignments. If two policies share a ranking letter assignment, then given the evaluations, they are not statistically distinguishable with 90%90\% confidence. Figure 3 also illustrates the anytime validity of AnyRank, where policy rankings are self-consistent at various total sample complexities even though fewer hypotheses are decided.
In contrast, Bradley–Terry methods do not provide FWER control. In RoboArena4 (Table 1), Classical and Davidson Bradley–Terry methods report incorrect rankings with high probability (miscalibration), scoring policy E higher than policy A (miscalibration). Given that AnyRank, using all available data, correctly ranks the policies with 90%90\% probability (see Table 3 and Figure 3(c)), we can state that there is a 90%90\% chance that Bradley-Terry ranking of E>A\textbf{E}>\textbf{A} is incorrect. Secondly, Bradley-Terry rankings need not be self-consistent as more evaluations are collected (inconsistency). This can be seen in Table 1, where all Bradley–Terry variants are self-inconsistent in RoboArena7. A second instance of inconsistency is when the number of policies are increased: Bradley-Terry (EM w/ partial progress) incorrectly concludes F>E\textbf{F}>\textbf{E} in Roboarena7 (at 5106 evaluations) despite using the same policy evaluation data for F and E as in RoboArena4. Additionally, we show a separate theoretical result on synthetic data — Bradley-Terry methods are, in general, inconsistent estimators; even with arbitrary amounts of evaluation data, they will not always converge to the correct ranking (see Section F.2.1). These examples on real evaluation data show that Bradley-Terry methods can produce incorrect rankings, highlighting the need for statistically reliable approaches. AnyRank fills this gap: it produces reliable rankings that remain consistent with varying number of policies and hardware evaluations.
Furthermore, AnyRank rankings in Table 1 use world model evaluations as a prior, which has low correlation with real-world empirical policy rankings for policies A−F\textbf{A}-\textbf{F}; the Spearman correlation between world model and real evaluations for these policies is r=0.05r=0.05 (see Figure 9). Despite these imperfect priors, AnyRank policy ranking is still guaranteed to be correct with high probability, and the sample complexity remains comparable to the no-prior ablation AnyRank0{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{0}.

Policy Evaluation Efficiency (R2).
Having established the importance of FWER control for reliable policy rankings, we show that AnyRank and its variants are efficient in comparison to Bonferroni and weighted Bonferroni. Figures 5 and 6 show the number of hypotheses rejected and sample complexity for RoboArena and TRI-LBM, respectively. Across all experiments, AnyRank and AnyRank0{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{0} reject the largest number of hypotheses with in the fewest number of samples. On RoboArena4 (with heldout evaluations as a prior), the AnyRank family achieves around a 50%50\% reduction in hardware trials to fully decide compared to Bonferroni (see Figure 4). Similar efficiency gains of AnyRank over Bonferroni hold for other RoboArena and TRI-LBM experiments (Figures 5 and 6).
In contrast, the Bonferroni baselines are less sample efficient, with weighted Bonferroni rejecting the fewest hypotheses despite having the highest sample complexity. This highlights the inefficiency of splitting risk budgets, this efficiency of AnyRank and its variants is enabled by the risk re-allocation of the underlying graphical testing methods.

AnyRank Improves with Good Priors (R3).
In experiments thus far, the performance of AnyRank and no-prior ablation AnyRank0{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{0} was comparable due to weak priors. Do strong priors improve the sample efficiency of AnyRank? For this, we consider the DROID-task series of experiments for the Fold towel task and use video world model finetuned on the specific task [10] to get strong policy ranking signals. We consider both diffusion policies and generalist policies with a range of empirical success rates as shown in Figure 10. Note that in this instance, the correlation between policy performance in the world model and the real world is high: r=0.89r=0.89. We use γ=10\gamma=10 to initialize the graph edge weights as well as the initial significance level assignments. In Figure 7, we see that AnyRank requires fewer samples than AnyRank0{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{0}, where no prior information is used and graph weights are set equally. Furthermore, since policy performance spans the spectrum, the lower bound on sample efficiency is by using a fixed sequence graph (AnyRank∞{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{\infty}) which has a total evaluation complexity of 188188 trials in this example. These results show that while strong priors can are helpful, the efficiency gains over no prior graphical versions need not be substantial. Instead, the bottleneck is that the number of hardware evaluations required for statistically reliable policy comparison can be large [20]. One approach to addressing this limitation is the use of proxy evaluations as a substitute for real evaluations [15], which we leave to future work.

However, as the number of policies increases, more policy pairs become difficult to distinguish, increasing the inherent difficulty of ranking for any statistically valid method. 6 Conclusion
Limitations and future work. Our framework assumes i.i.di.i.d evaluation data for providing statistical assurances. Evaluation best practices [1, 5] are particularly effective in this regard, some of which are incorporated into AnyRank, such as adaptive sampling that interleaves policy evaluations. In addition, reliably leveraging prior information to further reduce hardware evaluation effort while maintaining statistical assurances remains a challenge. In our investigation, existing simulation and off-the-shelf video world models priors are not sufficiently predictive of policy performance when evaluated at scale. In addition to developing effective priors for statistically rigorous evaluation at scale, a promising direction would be to reliably combine simulated and hardware evaluations for efficient, sequential hypothesis testing.
We propose AnyRank, a policy ranking framework that produces anytime-valid (partial) rankings, provides statistical assurances on their correctness, and achieves sample efficiency by adaptively allocating trials in a data-dependent manner and optionally leveraging prior information. This is made possible through novel theoretical contributions that fuse anytime-valid inference with multi-hypothesis testing. Experiments across real-world benchmark datasets demonstrate significant improvements in statistical reliability and sample efficiency compared to state-of-the-art approaches.

Appendix A Technical Implementation
A.1 Strategies for Graph Propagation and Risk Reallocation
We motivate the intuition for prior-dependent graph initialization using as illustrative comparison two special cases of multitest procedures: the Holm procedure [58] and the fixed-sequence procedure [18]. The Holm structure corresponds to uniform initial risk allocation and reallocation weights. It can be shown [58] that for KK hypotheses, the associated cutoffs at the time of decision for each rejected atomic hypothesis will be (in ordered form) {α[(n)]}≡{α∗/n:n∈{K,…,1}}\{\alpha^{[(n)]}\}\equiv\{\alpha^{*}/n:n\in\{K,...,1\}\}. This strictly dominates Bonferroni, as the total effective risk budget becomes ∑nα[(n)]=∑n(α∗/n)>α∗\sum_{n}\alpha^{[(n)]}=\sum_{n}(\alpha^{*}/n)>\alpha^{*}. As a concrete example, Figure 2 illustrates the Holm structure, with cutoffs {α[i]}={0.10,0.15,0.30}={α∗/i:i∈{3,2,1}}\{\alpha^{[i]}\}=\{0.10,0.15,0.30\}=\{\alpha^{*}/i:i\in\{3,2,1\}\}. In contrast, a fixed-sequence test corresponds to a chain graph in which the entire risk budget is initially assigned to a single hypothesis, which is then propagated to a single new hypothesis when a decision is made. When every hypothesis is decidable, this is the most efficient method, as the cutoffs at decision will be the least restrictive: α[i]=α∗ ∀i\alpha^{[i]}=\alpha^{*}\text{ }\forall i, exactly matching the theoretical upper bound. However, this approach is very committal – when not every hypothesis is decidable (e.g., when two policies have the same mean or, in finite samples, when the means are very close), fixed sequence methods will get stuck, often very early, and all subsequent hypotheses will fail to decide. A Holm-like procedure thus sacrifices some efficiency per hypothesis (α[i]<α∗\alpha^{[i]}<\alpha^{*}) to hedge against becoming stuck evaluating any one very hard comparison. In general, there is a continuum of graph specifications which trade off hedging and performance, represented by the initial risk allocation and reallocation weights. Selecting these weights (near-)optimally for policy ranking is the first central concern of this work.
A.2 Graph Initialization using a Prior
To initialize the graphical multiple testing procedure, we leverage prior estimates of policy performance differences. Let μ^\hat{\mu} denote empirical performance estimate (e.g., mean sucess rate or mean progress score) of a policy π\pi from simulation or world model rollouts, or heldout hardware evaluations. Suppose hypothesis ℋi\mathcal{H}_{i} involves the pairwise comparison of policies πAi\pi^{i}_{A} and πBi\pi^{i}_{B}. Let di=|μ^Ai−μ^Bi|d_{i}=|\hat{\mu}^{i}_{A}-\hat{\mu}^{i}_{B}| denote the magnitude of the predicted performance gap between the two policies compared by hypothesis ℋi\mathcal{H}_{i}.
A large did_{i} indicates a large predicted gap in policy performance. If this prediction is reflective of real-world performance, then the corresponding policies are easier to statistically distinguish, and the hypothesis ℋi\mathcal{H}_{i} can typically be decided with fewer evaluation trials on hardware. Therefore, an efficient algorithm will assign large α[i]\alpha^{[i]} to these hypotheses, in order that they decide quickly and can recycle the risk budget to other hypotheses. Conversely, when did_{i} is small, the policies are expected to exhibit similar performance, requiring more data to establish a statistically significant difference; as such, they should be assigned a smaller α[i]\alpha^{[i]}, from which they will accumulate recycled risk as easier evaluations terminate early.
We use the predicted performance gap to initialize both the local significance levels and the graph edge weights. Specifically, each hypothesis ℋi\mathcal{H}_{i} is assigned an initial significance level and outgoing graph edge-weights as follows:
α0[i]=α∗​exp⁡(γ​di)∑jexp⁡(γ​dj),w[i​j]=exp⁡(γ​dj)∑j≠iexp⁡(γ​dj),\alpha^{[i]}_{0}=\alpha^{*}\frac{\exp(\gamma d_{i})}{\sum_{j}\exp(\gamma d_{j})},\qquad w^{[ij]}=\frac{\exp(\gamma d_{j})}{\sum_{j\neq i}\exp(\gamma d_{j})},
(5)
where α∗\alpha^{*} is the global significance level and hyperparameter γ≥0\gamma\geq 0 controls the strength of the prior. Larger values of γ\gamma place a greater fraction of the significance budget on hypotheses with larger predicted performance differences. Similarly, the initial graph edge-weights are such that successor hypotheses ℋj\mathcal{H}_{j} with larger predicted performance gaps have higher weights w[i​j]w^{[ij]}. When ℋi\mathcal{H}_{i} is rejected, its local significance level α[i]\alpha^{[i]} gets redistributed to the remaining unresolved hypotheses in proportion to w[i​j]w^{[ij]}, with larger fractions going to successors ℋj\mathcal{H}_{j} that are predicted to be easier to decide. Allocating higher significance levels to these hypotheses further increases the chance that they are rejected early, thereby accelerating the redistribution of significance levels throughout the graph, and ultimately giving the hypotheses that are the most difficult to reject the highest possible significance level.
Note that if multiple hypotheses reject at the same time, the specific order of applying graph propagation does not matter [18].
At the same time, if the prior is inaccurate, the graphical procedure still remains valid and adapts its allocation through subsequent significance-level transfers, preserving family-wise error control regardless of the quality of the prior.

Appendix B Proof of Validity Under Transitive Closure
Three key components are required to demonstrate this property. First, we formalize the ranking problem in the lifted space of possible full rankings; second, we demonstrate that pairwise comparisons form a sufficient compression of this space so as to enable transitive closure; finally, we illustrate an equivalent decision rule in the lifted decision space that exactly recovers AnyRank with transitive closure. Thus, by showing that the decision rules are equivalent, we consequently demonstrate that adding transitive closure does not induce additional family-wise error.
B.1 The Lifted Representation
To simplify exposition, we label each policy in a ranking problem by a letter or set of letters (e.g, “A,” “B,” “C,” …). A comparison is explicitly represented by an inequality indicator, e.g, 𝟙[A>B]\mathbbm{1}[A>B]. A hypothesized ranking is represented by an ordered string. For example, A​B​C​D​E​FABCDEF is understood to represent the global ranking hypothesis “Policy A is better than Policy B, is better than Policy C, …” The formal candidate true policy ranking is represented by a boldface string, e.g., ABCDEF. Testing decision rules correspond to a mapping from evidence on belief rankings (e.g., A​B​C​D​E​FABCDEF) to decisions about candidates (e.g., ABCDEF). To make the exposition more concrete, we will carry through the practice of using six policies (i.e., the letters A through F); however, the argument generalizes to any number of policies, considering the rankings to essentially consist in strings of arbitrary length.
For KK policies, there are 2(K2)K\choose 2=K2−K=K^{2}-K pairwise comparisons. There are K!K! candidate true rankings. The important step is as follows: construct a ‘belief space’ over rankings consisting of the set of all hypothesized rankings consistent with each pairwise comparison. For each comparison (e.g., 𝟙[A>B]\mathbbm{1}[A>B]), precisely half of the candidate true rankings (i.e., K!/2K!/2) are consistent with the comparison, by a substitution argument.44
4
Specifically: every full ranking hypothesis must contain the constituent policies (here, AA and BB). For every ranking consistent with the hypothesis, (e.g., ABCDEF), an inconsistent can be constructed by substituting AA and BB (e.g., BACDEF). This will result in K!​(K2−K)/2K!(K^{2}-K)/2 belief space rankings, consisting in (K2−K)/2(K^{2}-K)/2 belief copies of each of the K!K! candidate rankings (by a similar substitution argument, each candidate true ranking is associated with belief copies for precisely half of the pairwise hypotheses, e.g. candidate ABCDEF has a belief copy A​B​C​D​E​FABCDEF associated with 𝟙[A>B]\mathbbm{1}[A>B], but not with 𝟙[B>A]\mathbbm{1}[B>A]).
Now, formally associate with each pairwise comparison the K!/2K!/2 hypothesized belief rankings with which is consistent in the form of a chain graph. For now, each chain graph can be ordered at random; in other words, the first and last elements of the chain do not matter. It is clear that, by construction, pairs of converse hypotheses (i.e., 𝟙[A>B]\mathbbm{1}[A>B] and 𝟙[B>A]\mathbbm{1}[B>A]) fully partition a copy of the full candidate true ranking set; as there are (K2)K\choose 2 pairs of converse hypotheses, each candidate true ranking again has (K2)K\choose 2 belief copies.
B.2 Transitive Closure as Set Inclusion
Understanding this partition idea, applying transitive closure amounts to considering relative set sizes of belief hypotheses induced by different combinations of pairwise comparisons.
In particular, consider the variable policy indices α,β,γ\alpha,\beta,\gamma, and denote by Σα>β\Sigma_{\alpha>\beta} the set of belief rankings implied by the hypothesis 𝟙[α>β]\mathbbm{1}[\alpha>\beta]. Again, to be concrete, A​B​C​D​E​F∈Σα=A>β=BABCDEF\in\Sigma_{\alpha=A>\beta=B}. We wish to show
(Σβ>α∪Σγ>β) ∩ Σγ>α=Σγ>α.(\Sigma_{\beta>\alpha}\cup\Sigma_{\gamma>\beta})\text{ }\cap\text{ }\Sigma_{\gamma>\alpha}=\Sigma_{\gamma>\alpha}.
(6)
Equivalently:
∀s∈Σγ>α,s∈(Σβ>α∪Σγ>β).\forall s\in\Sigma_{\gamma>\alpha},s\in(\Sigma_{\beta>\alpha}\cup\Sigma_{\gamma>\beta}).
(7)
The implication is that, if the above statement is true, then rejecting the transitive closure hypothesis γ>α\gamma>\alpha is implied by the two constituent decisions γ>β\gamma>\beta and β>α\beta>\alpha.
To show that the statement is in fact true, we use a pigeonhole argument on the relative position of β\beta: specifically, for any ranking s∈Σγ>αs\in\Sigma_{\gamma>\alpha}, it is the case that the ranking is of the form X¯​γ​Y¯​α​Z¯\underline{X}\gamma\underline{Y}\alpha\underline{Z}. Thus, we consider the enumerated possibilities β∈{X¯,Y¯,Z¯}\beta\in\{\underline{X},\underline{Y},\underline{Z}\}. Clearly, β∈X¯⟹β>α\beta\in\underline{X}\implies\beta>\alpha and β∈Z¯⟹γ>β\beta\in\underline{Z}\implies\gamma>\beta. Because β∈Y¯\beta\in\underline{Y} implies both, we see that any possible ranking in Σγ>α\Sigma_{\gamma>\alpha} implies at least one of γ>β\gamma>\beta and β>α\beta>\alpha. As such, we see that applying transitive closure does not increase the set of rejected hypotheses in the true ranking space; it only rejects copies of already-rejected belief rankings.
B.3 A Consistent AnyRank Decision Rule
We define the AnyRank decision rule as follows: reject candidate hypothesis if any of its belief copies rejects. Because each belief candidate is tied to precisely one pairwise comparison, this is equivalent to rejecting a possible ranking if and only if any pairwise implication is inconsistent with the accumulated evidence. This is exactly the implicit AnyRank decision rule! Thus, there is an AnyRank-consistent decision rule in the lifted belief space over full candidate rankings, which when implemented allows for transitive closure without increase in FWER. Thus, applying transitive closure as an AnyRank ‘wrapper’ does not imply rejection of a ranking candidate for which rejection was not already implied by preceding rejections; it only clarifies that, in the compressed space of pairwise comparisons, an additional hypothesis could indeed be rejected as a function of the underlying logic in the lifted space of candidate full rankings. For these reasons, FWER over formal rejection of candidate true rankings is not increased by the application of transitive closure. This completes the proof.
Appendix C Additional Technical Remarks
Remark 2 (IID Assumption).
Note that we do not assume i.i.d increments within policy rollouts, only across different task and environment draws. Without this assumption, policy comparison itself would not be well-defined, as expected success rate cannot be meaningfully compared under arbitrary distribution shifts. Separately, one would then have to make assumptions on the type of distribution shift, and there are usually too many possible forms of non-iid structure to characterize effects on FWER in general. Thus, the i.i.d assumption is nearly universally adopted in evaluation both across engineering disciplines and statistical methods (including confidence intervals and Bradley-Terry methods for robot evaluation). In robotics, there exist best practices to more closely realize the assumption [1, 5], and we briefly note that AnyRank’s adaptive sampling scheme implicitly integrates some of these practices, including alternating evaluations.
One can use non-iid detection to rigorously check for a broad class of FWER-relevant violations of the i.i.d. assumption, and prioritize detection of particular non-i.i.d. structure like mean drift (arising from camera bumps, mechanical wear, etc). We check the hardware evaluation data for each of the 34 policies considered in the paper; no policies show significant evidence of drift at level α=0.1\alpha=0.1, indicating the data in the paper does not demonstrate significant violation of the i.i.d assumption or performance degradation, which attenuates concerns of distribution shift in our specific context.
Remark 3 (One-sided vs two-sided tests).
Standard methods in multi-hypothesis correction require one-sided atomic null hypotheses. In our setting, one such hypothesis would be of the form: ℋ0:μi≥μj\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mu_{i}\geq\mu_{j}, ℋ1:μi<μj\mathcal{H}_{1}:\mu_{i}<\mu_{j}. Of course, a priori, we do not know which policy is better, necessitating the inclusion of the mirrored hypothesis ℋ0:μi≤μj\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mu_{i}\leq\mu_{j}, ℋ1:μi>μj\mathcal{H}_{1}:\mu_{i}>\mu_{j}. A potential issue arises with the inclusion of μi=μj\mu_{i}=\mu_{j} in both null hypotheses. This means that, when the means are exactly equal, they belong to the null in each direction; thus, any declarative ranking decision is incorrect (under the convention that ranking implies non-equality). In this setting, if the probability that either one-sided hypothesis rejects is set to α\alpha, then the probability of an incorrect decision when the means are equal is bounded at 2​α2\alpha. As such, a union bound correction (i.e., running each one-sided test at α/2\alpha/2) is in general required. If the convention is taken that equality allows either ranking decision to be correct, this is not necessary. Throughout the paper, we present results without this correction, under the reasoning that it is highly unlikely that the complex distribution over tasks and environments would ever induce exactly identical means for a policy pair. However, the results can be easily adapted to apply this two-sided correction by instantiating the procedure at significance level α/2\alpha/2.
Remark 4 (Evidence Threshold).
Each α[i]\alpha^{[i]} can be understood as controlling the ‘evidence threshold’ for a pairwise comparison: a higher α[i]\alpha^{[i]} corresponds to a looser threshold, generally requiring fewer trials before the hypothesis can be rejected. As such, deflating each hypothesis test’s significance level by a factor of KK increases requisite hardware evaluations, limiting the statistical power and sample efficiency of the ranking procedure. Thus, our goal is to maximize statistical power by finding looser cutoffs that still guarantee FWER control, thereby deciding as many atomic hypotheses as possible while minimizing the hardware evaluation effort required to do so.
Remark 5 (Statistical Power).
For single hypothesis testing, β∈[0,1]\beta\in[0,1] corresponds to the probability of a failed detection under the alternative. In our multihypothesis setting, we account for partial rankings by assigning β∈[0,1]\beta\in[0,1] as the expected fraction of atomic hypotheses which fail to reject (in a given sampling budget), where (1−β)(1-\beta) is the fraction rejected (statistical power). The ‘full ranking power’ 1−βF​R∈[0,1]1-\beta_{FR}\in[0,1] can be defined as the probability that all atomic hypotheses reject (i.e, that we recover a full ranking). It will always be the case that (1−βF​R)≤(1−β)(1-\beta_{FR})\leq(1-\beta). This can be seen by defining X∈[0,1]X\in[0,1] to be the random variable denoting the fraction of rejected hypotheses; then, 1−βF​R=𝔼⁡[⌊X⌋]≤𝔼⁡[X]=1−β1-\beta_{FR}=\mathbb{E}\big[\lfloor X\rfloor\big]\leq\mathbb{E}\big[X\big]=1-\beta.
Appendix D Choice of Baselines
FWER-controlling Baselines.
We compare against a (sequentialized) standard Bonferroni correction, which controls for the global risk level α∗\alpha^{*} by assigning each atomic hypothesis test a portion of the risk budget and controlling the overall error rate via a union bound.
This approach was used for rigorous multi-policy comparison in LBM policy evaluations [5]. In addition, we also compare against a weighted Bonferroni method, in which atomic hypotheses that are predicted to be difficult to decide based on the prior receive a larger fraction of the significance budget. While Holm [58] and fixed sequence [55] are generally more efficient in the batch regime, they do not admit simple extensions to the sequential setting, unlike Bonferroni. Introducing sequential versions of these tests (as AnyRank0{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{0} and AnyRank∞{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{\infty}, respectively) for the purposes of active evaluation, and proving their FWER control, is the first core contribution of our work. This contribution precisely enables risk recycling for active evaluation and more efficient evaluation decision-making; the comparison of AnyRank0{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{0} and AnyRank∞{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{\infty} to Bonferroni approaches directly measures this benefit.
Furthermore, AnyRank0{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{0} and AnyRank∞{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{\infty} also act as an ablation for the benefits of prior information, the second core contribution of this work.
Thus, we quantify these benefits by comparing the fully general AnyRankγ{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{\gamma} to AnyRank0{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{0} and AnyRank∞{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\textsc{AnyRank}}_{\infty}; we then quantify the benefits of risk recycling by comparing the latter approaches to sequentialized (weighted) Bonferroni approaches. Per the preceding footnote, we then emphasize that all of these considered approaches already implicitly bake in significant benefits with respect to any and all batch evaluation procedures.
As a second fair baseline for the benefits of using prior information, we also instantiate a weighted Bonferroni procedure in which the initial risk allocation is in inverse proportion to the predicted difficulty of comparison did_{i}, to inform understanding of the benefits of graph asymmetry independent of the risk recycling mechanism:

Appendix F Synthetic Experiments
On real-world evaluations, the true mean of task progress of a policy is unknown. Given this lack of access to the true labels, one cannot assess FWER Control or Asymptotic Inconsistency. For real data, we can partially validate Miscalibration of baseline procedures via comparison of atomic hypotheses to AnyRank, which is guaranteed to be correct with probability 1−α1-\alpha. Additionally, we can assess Inconsistency by running procedures on data subsets of varying sizes, both in the number of trials as well as in the number of policies. To validate the remaining properties, we require the construction of synthetic data for which oracle knowledge of the true underlying means is known, in order to validate using the true labels over many independent evaluation datasets that FWER Control and Asymptotic Consistency are jointly maintained in general.
F.1 Ranking Procedures
Bradley-Terry Variants.
As in Section 5, in these experiments we use a standard Bradley-Terry procedure (“BT (Classical)” in Table 1), as well as the variant constructed in [2] (“BT (EM)”). These methods are hypothesized to not control FWER, nor to be consistent estimators for general problems of ranking mean policy performance.
Bootstrapped Bradley-Terry Approaches.
In order to (asymptotically) ensure FWER control in the BT procedures, we implement a bootstrapped BT for each batch. To bootstrap, we begin with a BT variant (described in the preceding subsection) and generate M=5000M=5000 redraws i.i.d. with replacement from the batch dataset, with each redraw of size equal to the nominal batch dataset, NN, and take the empirical {α/(2​K),1−α/(2​K)}\{\alpha/(2K),1-\alpha/(2K)\} quantiles of the BT scores for each policy independently. We then compare the policies pairwise by utilizing these confidence intervals; any pair of intervals with no overlap correspond to a significant rejection. A Bonferroni correction over these pairwise comparisons guarantees (asymptotic) 1−α1-\alpha FWER control. Unfortunately, because these intervals are constructed in a brittle way, attempting to utilize graphical methods or other more efficient multitest corrections is infeasible, because it would require computing a combinatorial number of different confidence intervals.
In this setting, we hypothesize that for most problems, the rankings will be FWER-robust in that decisions will only be made if there is significant evidence. However, we hypothesize that it will result in a significant loss of statistical power. Additionally, we hypothesize that this variant will inherit the inconsistency properties from the standard BT variants.

F.2 Experiments with Synthetic Data
The central limitation of Bradley-Terry procedures is the difficulty of ascertaining a level of Type-1 Error on practical datasets. For instances in which the underlying metric of interest is unobservable, BT often remains the best option. However, in the robotics evaluation context, BT is often used to map evaluator preference to rankings of overall performance; this setting is therefore fundamentally different, because robot performance is directly observed in the evaluation rubric.55
5
An analogy: one might use BT methods to attempt to ‘power rank’ athletic performance when an absolute and shared performance measure is not present. This is the case in sports driven by win-loss records – soccer, baseball, basketball, etc. The observed quantities (wins and losses) are only proxies for the quantity of interest (ability, quality of play, etc). This mismatch necessitates BT-type rectification. By contrast, one would not use BT to try to rank runners in track events like the 100m dash, because the win-loss measure is derivative from a shared, global measure – the race times themselves are directly observed! In the robotics context, preferences may be a useful proxy for some estimation problems, but do not make sense as a proxy for empirical performance because we also directly observe empirical performance. The reader may again contrast this with LLM-based evaluation where BT was introduced to the robotics context. There, the measure of response quality is imprecise, as many responses equally satisfy the query. As a result, properties like FWER control become far more important, as there are many plausible methods to construct a ranking.
To validate Type-1 Error (FWER) control, we run AnyRank, Bradley-Terry (BT), and Bootstrapped Bradley-Terry (BTα) on many instances of synthetic Bernoulli data. This data structure corresponds to the setting of ranking policies by task success rate. By running many independent instances of ranking problems, we can validate the properties of the ranking algorithm (which in general cannot be done on real datasets).
For each synthetic policy ranking instance, the maximal number of (fictitious) evaluations per policy is set as Nm​a​x=2000N_{max}=2000. The global ranking is FWER controlled at level α=0.05\alpha=0.05.

F.2.1 Limitations of Bradley–Terry: Asymptotic Inconsistency
On synthetic data, we illustrate a separate and important phenomenon of applying BT procedures for ranking: the estimated Bradley-Terry ranking may converge to an incorrect ordering, even with infinite data and when directly using empirical performance comparison as the preference rule. The last policy is low-variance, achieving scores of 0.80.8 and 0.90.9 with equal probability. We now show that BT methods are inconsistent in this case: that is, in the infinite-data limit, any BT method using empirical performance as the preference indicator will rank the policies inconsistently with the actual order of said policies’ means. F.3 Validating Type-1 Error (FWER) Control on Synthetic Data
We begin with case HO-I, in which two policies have the same mean and therefore cannot be ordered (under the convention that ordering μi\mu_{i} vs μj\mu_{j} implies that μi≠μj\mu_{i}\neq\mu_{j}). In this setting, nine of the ten hypotheses are decidable (resulting in a partial ranking). In Figure 13 we illustrate the FWER control of both AnyRank and BTα (i.e., bootstrapped BT) at level α∗=0.05\alpha^{*}=0.05 (solid lines). In this setting, a full ranking corresponds to an error, as it implies we have ordered the two policies that have the same mean; therefore, any instance in which that occurs is a Type-1, or Family-Wise, error. Consequently, the solid lines correspond to the Type-1 Error rate. As can be observed, our method uses nearly the full risk budget (an empirical error rate of 0.0450.045), while BTα is safe, but much more conservative.

F.4 Note: Empirical Performance is as Strong as Non-Bootstrapped BT
We briefly develop the analogy given in Footnote 5 by considering the efficacy of preference-based BT (using preferences based on empirical performance) vis-a-vis a related non-FWER controlling approach: ranking by empirical performance. In Figure 16, Figure 18, and Figure 17 we show that across data scales in the synthetic setting, using the current empirical means to rank the policies is as effective on aggregate as preference-based BT rankings, when FWER control is not a concern. This result reinforces the notion that, when the ultimate metric of interest is directly observable, it is generally optimal to estimate that metric based on the direct constituent observations of the metric, and not on proxy measures.77
7
Assuming, for example, that the relative data availability is similar, etc. Badithela et al. [15] is an example where the incorporation of a cheaper simulation proxy saves evaluation on more expensive hardware. In our setting, by contrast, two policy rollouts yield either a preference score or two evaluation rollout scores at approximately equal cost.

F.6 Counterexample and Bradley-Terry Variants
We repeat the empirical validation on the counterexample presented in the preceding section, but using the specific EM variant of Bradley-Terry procedures presented in [2]. Using the bootstrap modification on dataset sizes of 1000 comparison draws, we observe failed FWER control on five of six simulation reruns (with indeterminate ranking on the remaining run) at nominal level α=0.05\alpha=0.05; this is significant at δ=0.001\delta=0.001 against the hypothesis that boostrapped EM Bradley-Terry is indeed maintaining FWER control at level α=0.05\alpha=0.05, via the use of binomial confidence interval-induced tests [53]. As such, the EM Bradley-Terry procedure also fails to correctly rank the policies by mean performance, despite consistent ranking selection at all times. This illustrates clearly the flattening effect of comparison-based approaches – the magnitude of performance differences can be lost, which can skew results significantly when those magnitudes are skewed or asymmetric.
