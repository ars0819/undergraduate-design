ABSTRACT
Vision-language-action (VLA) models offer a promising path toward general-purpose robot manipulation. While
inference latency can affect task success in a constantly evolving physical world, the relationship between latency
and task success rate remains poorly understood. In this paper, we introduce VLALAGBENCH, a comprehensive
benchmark for studying VLA inference latency sensitivity across task types, object speeds, execution modes
(synchronous and asynchronous), and VLA policies. Using VLALAGBENCH, we find that even a modest
inference latency of 100 ms can nearly halve task success on moving targets, with sensitivity varying substantially
across tasks and policies. These differences reflect task-specific requirements for precision and continuous motion.
By overlapping inference with action execution, asynchronous execution can recover some of the lost success
at the cost of more frequent inference requests. Building on these findings, we develop an empirical model that
predicts task success from limited calibration data, achieving root-mean-square prediction errors of only 5.7–7.8%
across various VLA policies. Given an inference system’s latency and per-robot throughput, these predictions
guide the selection of system-specific VLA algorithm configurations to maximize task success. We believe
VLALAGBENCHand these findings provide a solid foundation for future robotics algorithm–system co-design.
1 INTRODUCTION
Physical AI has the potential to enable robots to perform di-
verse tasks autonomously in the real world. Recent advances
in vision-language-action (VLA) models have brought this
goal closer by predicting robot actions via visual observa-
tions and natural-language instructions. These models have
demonstrated strong capabilities across a wide range of
manipulation tasks, highlighting their potential for general-
purpose robotics (Black et al., 2025a; NVIDIA et al., 2025;
Fang et al., 2026).
Although VLA models need to respond in real time to keep
up with changes in the physical world, the inference latency
required for successful task execution remains poorly un-
derstood. From a systems perspective, VLA-Perf (Jiang
et al., 2026) uses 10 Hz and 100 Hz as reference points for
acceptable and high-performance inference, respectively,
without evaluating how these rates translate into task suc-
cess. Similarly, Realtime-VLA (Ma et al., 2025; Yang et al.,
2026a; Niu et al., 2026) improves inference and execution
speed, but demonstrates the benefits on a limited set of
tasks, such as catching a falling pen. From a benchmark-
ing perspective, popular robotic manipulation benchmarks
such as LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) and RoboCasa (Nasiri-
any et al., 2024) primarily evaluate model generalization
across tasks and scenes, but their static environments pro-
vide limited insight into how inference latency affects task
success in evolving physical environments. While recent
benchmarks such as DynamicVLA’s DOM, MoveBench,
and ReflexBench (Xie et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Chen
et al., 2026b) support manipulation tasks with moving ob-
jects, these benchmarks do not systematically evaluate how
inference latency affects task success: their evaluations ei-
ther omit latency, use latencies tied to specific hardware, or
cover only a limited set of policies and tasks.
In this paper,we present VLALAGBENCH, a bench-
mark for systematically studying how VLA inference
latency affects task success, which provides the founda-
tion for future robotic system design. As shown in Fig-
ure 1, VLALAGBENCHexamines five factors related to
latency sensitivity. (1)Task type: grasping a moving ob-
ject, releasing an object into a moving receptacle, placing
an object on a moving surface, and manipulating a moving
articulated object. (2)Object speed: different target speeds
within each task. (3)VLA policy: popular models including
π0.5, GR00T N1.7, and MolmoAct2 (Black et al., 2025a;
NVIDIA, 2026a; Fang et al., 2026). (4)Execution mode:

Our evaluation with VLALAGBENCHyields fourkey find-
ings, each addressing a research question:
Q1. How does inference latency affect task success across
different manipulation tasks?
Latency sensitivity varies substantially across tasks (§4.2).
For objects moving at 0.1 m/s, a moderate inference latency
of 100 ms nearly halves success in one task (from 71.5% to
40.5%), while another retains more (86%) of its zero-latency
success rate (from 86.5% to 74.0%).
Q2. What factors determine a task’s sensitivity to infer-
ence latency?
Latency reduces task success through (1) stale observations
(the target has moved by the time inference completes) and
(2) robot pauses during synchronous inference. Their impact
varies by task: stale observations affect tasks that particu-
larly require precise manipulation, while pauses disrupt
tasks requiring continuous motion (§4.2). These factors
motivate our next study of asynchronous execution.
Q3. What are the trade-offs between synchronous and
asynchronous execution as inference latency increases?
Asynchronous execution overlaps inference with robot ac-
tions, keeping the robot moving and benefiting tasks that
require continuous motion. When inference latency is suf-
ficiently short, continuous asynchronous execution also re-
duces observation staleness through more frequent action
updates, improving precision. These benefits come at the
cost of more frequent inference requests (§4.3).
Q4. Do different VLA models exhibit the same latency
sensitivity?
VLA policies differ in their sensitivity to stale observations
and robot pauses, thus benefiting to different extents from
asynchronous execution. Additionally, the best-performing
policy at a given inference latency depends on both the task
and target speed (§4.4).
Building on these findings,we further develop an empir-
ical model that guides the selection of algorithm con-
figurations for a given inference system(§5). Different
combinations of GPUs, networks, and software runtimes re-
sult in different inference latencies and per-robot throughput.
Consequently, the VLA configuration, including the execu-
tion mode and action execution horizon, that maximizes task
success may differ across systems. Because exhaustively
evaluating this configuration space is prohibitively expen-
sive, we develop a model that estimates task success across
latencies, execution modes, and execution horizons, using
only a small set of measurements. Given a system’s latency
and throughput constraints, we thus use these predictions to
select the feasible algorithm configuration with the highest
predicted task success.
This paper makes three maincontributions, which together
provide a solid foundation for future robotics algorithm–
system co-designs:
• We introduce VLALAGBENCHto comprehensively
benchmark how VLA latency affects task success.
• We present findings on latency sensitivity across tasks,
target speeds, execution modes, and VLA policies.
• We develop an empirical model that predicts task suc-
cess rate and use it to guide algorithm configuration
choice given a user-provided system specification.

2.1 Vision-Language-Action Models
Vision-language-action (VLA) models represent an impor-
tant step toward physical AI by using visual observations
and natural-language instructions to generate robot actions
for continuous control (Black et al., 2025b;a; NVIDIA et al.,
2025). As shown in Figure 2, a VLA model combines a
vision-language model (VLM) backbone that encodes cam-
era images and language instructions with an action expert
that predicts the robot’s next sequence of actions. Models
such as π0, π0.5, GR00T N1, and MolmoAct2 use diffusion
transformers (DiT) as action experts, transforming sampled
noise into continuous action sequences through multiple
refinement iterations (Black et al., 2025b;a; NVIDIA et al.,
2025; Fang et al., 2026). OpenVLA and the original Mol-
moAct instead generate discrete action tokens autoregres-
sively, which are then decoded into robot actions (Kim et al.,
2025; Lee et al., 2026).
Modern VLA policies commonly predict anaction chunk,
a sequence of future actions generated in one inference
call (Zhao et al., 2023; Black et al., 2025b; NVIDIA et al.,
2025; Fang et al., 2026). We denote the number of pre-
dicted actions byK, theaction chunk size, and the number
executed before switching to an updated chunk by H, the
execution horizon(Black et al., 2025c). Predicting multi-
ple actions together helps maintain consistent motion, while
executing only a shorter prefix (H < K ) lets the robot incor-
porate new observations and adjust its actions sooner (Chi
et al., 2023; Black et al., 2025c). Figure 3 shows an ex-
ample of two chunks generated by separate inference calls
(assuming zero inference latency): the robot executes the
first H= 5 actions of chunk A (K= 10 ), then switches to
chunk B, replacing A’s remaining actions.

objects, goals, and scenes. For example, LIBERO provides
task suites that vary spatial relationships, object identities,
and task goals, together with a long-horizon suite that re-
quires executing sequences of manipulation skills (Liu et al.,
2023). RoboCasa expands evaluation to diverse kitchen
environments, covering individual skills such as pick-and-
place and opening drawers, as well as composite activi-

ties that combine multiple skills (Nasiriany et al., 2024).
RoboLab evaluates generalist policies on household manip-

These benchmarks provide valuable measures of task com-
petence and generalization, but limited insight into the con-
sequences of inference latency. Due to the static physical en-
vironments in these benchmarks, a longer inference latency
increases task completion time without directly affecting
the task outcome (Chen et al., 2026b). Consequently, these
benchmarks do not reflect whether a policy can react quickly
enough to succeed when the physical world continues to
evolve during inference.
2.3 Dynamic Manipulation Benchmarks
Recent dynamic manipulation benchmarks introduce mov-
ing objects to test policy reactivity, but they do not sys-
tematically isolate how inference latency degrades task suc-
cess. For example, MoveBench tests grasping under con-
stant, accelerating, and irregular object velocities (Zhang
et al., 2026), DynamicVLA’s Dynamic Object Manipula-
tion (DOM) benchmark evaluates grasping and placing with
moving targets (Xie et al., 2026), and ReflexBench intro-
duces time-critical tasks such as conveyor picking and ball
catching (Chen et al., 2026b). However, MoveBench does
not account for inference latency during simulation (Zhang
et al., 2026). DOM runs the simulator during inference,
but its latencies are tied to the policy and GPU used for
evaluation, without a controlled sweep showing how task
success changes with inference latency (Xie et al., 2026).
ReflexBench explicitly varies inference latency under syn-
chronous and asynchronous execution, but studies its effect
using a single policy (SmolVLA) on only two tasks (Chen
et al., 2026b).
Research gap.Because neither existing benchmarks nor
latency-aware model papers (Appendix H) jointly vary task
types, object speeds, execution modes, and generalist VLA
policies under controlled inference latency, the systematic
relationship between VLA latency and robot task success
rate remains uncharacterized.

3 BENCHMARK ANDEXECUTIONMODES
We present VLALAGBENCH, a benchmark for systemati-
cally studying how VLA inference latency affects task suc-
cess. As summarized in Figure 1, we vary five factors: (1)
manipulation task type, (2) target object speed, (3) VLA pol-
icy, (4) execution mode, and (5) inference latency. We hope
the insights from the systematic evaluation of VLALAG-
BENCH(§4) will guide future robotic system and architec-
ture designs.
3.1 Design Principles
To ensure that performance drops reflect latency sensitivity
rather than baseline policy failures or simulator artifacts,
VLALAGBENCHis built around four design principles.
First, evaluated policies must achieve high success rate in
static scenes at zero latency so that accuracy degradation
with added latency is attributable to latency. Second, the
tasks should cover diverse interactions with moving targets,
such as grasping a moving object or placing an object at a
moving destination. Third, object moving trajectories must
be precisely controllable without altering the simulator’s
underlying contact physics. Fourth, multiple widely adopted
VLA models must provide public checkpoints on the exact
same tasks to enable cross-policy comparisons.
These principles rule out most of the widely used sim-
ulated manipulation benchmarks we screened. The first
principle alone excludes several benchmarks because pub-
lic VLA checkpoints have limited success even in static
scenes. For example, π0.5 averages only 16.9% success on
RoboCasa365 (Nasiriany et al., 2026) and 28.0% on Robo-
Lab (Yang et al., 2026b). Similarly, π0 averages 47.9% on
Meta-World (Yu et al., 2020; Shukor et al., 2025) and 46.4%
on the clean scenes of RoboTwin 2.0’s bimanual tasks (Chen
et al., 2026a), while GR00T N1.7 averages 62.3% on Sim-
plerEnv’s WidowX scenes (Li et al., 2025; NVIDIA, 2026b).
Kinetix (Matthews et al., 2025) fails the second and fourth
principle: its 2D physics control tasks are evaluated using
small policies trained per environment rather than VLA
models (Black et al., 2025c).
Thus, we build our primary benchmark on LIBERO in
MuJoCo, which satisfies our design principles and allows
controlled target motion while preserving realistic contact
physics (Todorov et al., 2012). We use DOM in Isaac Sim
for secondary cross-simulator verification (Xie et al., 2026;
NVIDIA, n.d.) (Appendix E). We also verify our findings
through real-robot experiments (Appendix F).
3.2 Tasks
We select four representative tasks that cover distinct ways
a robot interacts with a moving target (Figure 4).
(a) Release.The robot picks up a stationary cream cheese
package and drops it into a moving basket.
(b) Set-down.The robot picks up a stationary bowl and
places it on a moving plate.
(c) Grasping.The robot picks up a bowl carried by a mov-
ing cabinet and places it on a stationary plate.
(d) Articulated manipulation.The robot grasps the handle
of the moving cabinet’s middle drawer and pulls it open.
We select these four tasks because they achieve near-perfect
policy success in static scenes, show distinct robot–object
interactions, and are sensitive to target motion. Appendix A
explains why other LIBERO tasks were not selected.
These tasks differ in how the robot interacts with the moving
target.Releaseandset-downdeliver an object to a moving
destination, whereasgraspingandarticulated-object ma-
nipulationrequire the robot to acquire a moving object or
handle directly. For the moving destination tasks, release al-
lows coarse alignment over a wide basket opening, whereas

tasks as the primary benchmark and report DynamicVLA
as a cross-simulator verification in Appendix E.
3.3 Motion Parameters
Each target moves at a constant speed along a closed, nearly
elliptical path that avoids collisions with background objects
in LIBERO’s original layouts. We sweep target speeds
from 0.001 m/s (near-static) to 0.2 m/s across all four tasks;
Appendix B provides trajectory and initialization details.
3.4 VLA Policies
VLALAGBENCHevaluates three representative VLA poli-
cies: π0.5, GR00T N1.7, and MolmoAct2, using their
official public checkpoints (Physical Intelligence, 2025;
NVIDIA, 2026a; Fang et al., 2026). These checkpoints
achieve near-perfect success in static scenes, providing a re-
liable baseline for comparing latency sensitivity across poli-
cies. For cross-simulator verification on DOM in Isaac Sim,
we evaluate DynamicVLA, a policy explicitly trained on
moving objects on the DOM benchmark (Xie et al., 2026).

3.5 Execution Modes
We compare synchronous execution with two representative
asynchronous schedules: lazy and continuous execution
(Figure 5). An execution mode governs when the robot
requests a new action chunk and how it acts while waiting,
creating a fundamental trade-off between robot idle time,
observation staleness, and inference frequency. Across the
three modes, K denotes the number of predicted actions
per chunk, H the number executed per chunk, the execution
horizon, and L the inference latency expressed as a number
of control steps (§3.6).
Mode 1: Synchronous execution.Synchronous execu-
tion is a common deployment pattern in which inference
and robot action execution interleaves (Black et al., 2025c).
After executing H actions, the robot requests a new chunk
and pauses for L control steps while inference completes,
holding its position and last gripper command. Once the ac-
tion chunk becomes available, the robot resumes execution
from its first predicted action.
Pros and cons.Its advantage is its low inference request rate:
one request every H+L control steps. Its disadvantage is
that the robot pauses during every inference call, which can
disrupt tasks that require continuous motion.
Idle time.We quantify robot pauses using idle time ( I), the
number of control steps spent waiting for inference per H
executed actions. Synchronous execution incurs I=L idle
steps per execution horizon (hatched in Figure 5). Once the
first action chunk is available, both asynchronous modes
introduced below eliminate pauses ( I= 0 ), provided the
available actions cover the next inference interval.

Mode 2: Lazy asynchronous execution.Asynchronous
execution avoids robot pauses by running inference while
the robot executes actions from an earlier chunk. Lazy
execution requests inference least frequently among various
asynchronous schedules, starting the next call only when L
of the H scheduled actions remain so that the next chunk
becomes available as the current execution horizon ends.
When the new chunk arrives, the robot skips its first L
actions, which correspond to the elapsed inference interval,
and executes the followingHactions.
Pros and cons.Its advantage is that it keeps the robot
moving continuously without pauses. Its disadvantages
are that (1) more frequent inference does not reduce average
observation staleness relative to synchronous execution, and
(2) robot motion during inference can introduce additional
uncertainty for tasks requiring precise manipulation.
Observation staleness.We define observation staleness as
the average time elapsed between capturing a visual ob-
servation and executing an action generated from it. As
illustrated in Figure 5, synchronous execution runs actions0
through H−1 of each chunk, yielding an average staleness
of (H−1)/2 +L with an inference latency of L steps. Be-
cause lazy asynchronous execution skips the first L actions,
its observation staleness is identically (H−1)/2 +L steps.
Mode 3: Continuous asynchronous execution.Con-
tinuous execution represents the opposite extreme in asyn-
chronous execution, running inference as frequently as pos-
sible by starting each new call as soon as the previous one
completes, while the robot continues executing previously
predicted actions. At each completion, the robot switches
to the new chunk, skips its firstLactions, and executes the
next L actions while another inference call runs, yielding
an execution horizon of H=L and an average observation
staleness of(3L−1)/2steps.
Pros and cons.Its advantage is more frequent action updates
and thus a lower observation staleness than in either of the
other modes when latency is shorter than their execution
horizon (L <5 ). The average staleness is even lower than
in zero-latency synchronous execution with H= 5 when
L= 1 . Its disadvantages are a higher inference request rate
and more frequent transitions between chunks, which can
introduce discontinuities in the robot’s actions.
3.6 Latency Injection Mechanism
We simulate inference latency by converting it into a number
of simulator control steps. For example, at a control fre-
quency of 20 Hz, a latency of 100 ms corresponds toL= 2
steps: synchronous execution inserts two stationary actions
that hold the robot’s position and last gripper command,
whereas asynchronous execution continues executing avail-
able actions from the previous chunk. We extend each task’s
deadline by the number of inference-induced idle steps to
preserve its action budget (Appendix B).
4 EVALUATION ANDFINDINGS
In this section, we use VLALAGBENCHto (1) examine how
inference latency affects success across tasks, (2) identify
the factors leading to accuracy loss, (3) assess the trade-
offs between synchronous and asynchronous execution, and
(4) compare latency sensitivity across VLA policies. We
present the main findings here, with the full experimental
setup, complete results, failure stages, and validation in
Isaac Sim with DynamicVLA (Appendix E) and on real
robots (Appendix F) provided in the appendices.
4.1 Evaluation setup
On LIBERO-based tasks, we test three widely used VLA
models, including π0.5, GR00T N1.7, and MolmoAct2 at
a 20 Hz control rate with an execution horizon of H= 5
and their default chunk sizes of K= 10 (π0.5, MolmoAct2)
or K= 16 (GR00T). Each reported success rate is based
on 200 rollouts, with a budget of 600 executed actions per
episode. This increased budget, up from 220–300 actions
in standard static LIBERO, reduces failures caused by in-
sufficient time to pursue moving targets. We test inference
latencies from 0 to 250 ms in 50 ms increments because (1)
this range covers latencies encountered in practical VLA
inference systems (Xu et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026), and
(2) at 250 ms ( L= 5 ), policies with K= 10 reach the
asynchronous execution limitK=H+L.
4.2 Latency sensitivity across tasks
Q1.How does inference latency affect task success across
different manipulation tasks?
Finding 1.Inference latency can substantially reduce
task success on moving targets, with the extent of degrada-
tion depending on both the task nature and target speed.
Empirical success curves confirm that latency penalties
emerge primarily when targets move and scale differently
across tasks. As shown in Figure 6, an inference latency
of 200 ms under synchronous execution changes success
on nearly static targets (0.001 m/s) by only a few percent-
age points across all tasks. On moving targets, however,
even moderate inference latency affects tasks differently: at
a target speed of 0.1 m/s, 100 ms of latency nearly halves
set-downsuccess, from 71.5% to 40.5% (57% of its zero-
latency success rate). In contrast,graspingsuccess declines
only from 86.5% to 74.0%, retaining 86% of its zero-latency
success rate. We next examine the factors underlying these
differences in latency sensitivity across tasks.

Q2.What factors determine a task’s sensitivity to latency?
Finding 2.A task’s latency sensitivity depends on its
requirements for action precision and continuous motion.
(2.1) Tasks requiring high spatial precision suffer from stale
observations, whereas (2.2) tasks requiring sustained mo-
tion suffer from robot pauses.
(2.1) Precision.Stale observations make precise manipula-
tion harder because the target moves between image capture
and action execution, so the robot may act on a position the
target no longer occupies. Figure 7 compares the precision
required by different tasks:set-downrequires placing the
bowl within a narrow 3 cm zone around the plate’s center,
whereasreleasetolerates drops anywhere across a 12.2 cm
basket opening. At a target speed of 0.1 m/s, 200 ms of addi-
tional observation staleness allows the plate to move 2 cm, a
displacement equal to two-thirds ofset-down’s tolerance for
placement error, making precise placement more difficult.
(2.2) Continuous motion.Robot pauses disrupt tasks that
require continuous motion, making it harder to catch up

5 SYSTEM-SPECIFICCONFIGURATION
SELECTION WITHVLALAGBENCH
In this section, we build on the preceding findings to guide
VLA configuration selection for a given inference system.
Specifically, we develop an empirical model that uses lim-
ited calibration data for each policy–task pair to predict
task success across inference latencies, execution modes,
and execution horizons (§5.1). This model reuses measure-
ments collected on one system to select configurations that
maximize predicted task success on a new system (§5.2).
5.1 An Empirical Model for Task Success Prediction
Success model intuition.Stale observations and robot
pauses caused by inference latency can make a task harder,
much like increasing the target’s speed. This similarity
suggests a way to reuse past success measurements: we first
measure how task success varies with target speed under
one latency and execution mode. To estimate success under
another latency and execution modes, we simply look up
the success rate on the measured curve at a target speed
adjusted for observation staleness and robot idle time.
For a given policy and task, we approximate success under
configuration X as SX(v)≈S 0(λX v), where S0 is the suc-
cess curve under zero-latency synchronous execution with
a fixed reference horizon. Configuration X specifies the
inference latency, execution mode, and execution horizon.
The factor λX adjusts the target speed: to predict success
at speed v, we read the reference curve at speed λX v. In
practice, we find that this adjustment is well approximated
by a simple linear model of observation staleness and robot

5.2 System-Oriented Algorithm Space Exploration
Achieving the highest task success rate on a given inference
system requires choosing the right combination of execution
mode and execution horizon. The GPU, network, software
runtime, and resource-sharing arrangement jointly deter-
mine the system’s inference latency and available through-
put per robot, so a configuration that performs well on one
system may not be the best choice for another. Explor-
ing this configuration space experimentally is expensive
because each candidate requires repeated task executions to
estimate its success rate.Our goal is therefore to reuse past
deployment data to select configurations that maximize task
success on new systems.
We apply the prediction model from §5.1 to four repre-
sentative system deployment scenarios (Table 3). These
comprise an onboard GPU with relatively high inference
latency (System A), a dedicated edge server with low la-
tency (System B), a shared server with low latency but
limited inference throughput per robot (System C), and a
remote cloud server with high network latency (System D).

6 CONCLUSION ANDFUTUREWORK
VLALAGBENCHrepresents a foundational step toward un-
derstanding how VLA inference latency affects task success.
We identify the factors that shape latency sensitivity and
compare execution modes and VLA policies across tasks
and target speeds. We further develop an empirical model
that predicts task success and guides execution-mode selec-
tion for a given inference system. We hope these findings
will provide useful guidance for future robotics algorithm–
system co-design solutions.

A TASK SELECTION WITHINLIBERO
We selected the four tasks from LIBERO’s four standard
suites by three criteria: the evaluated checkpoints were
trained on the task’s suite and succeed in nearly all of its
static episodes, each task adds an interaction the others lack,
and moving the target lowers success enough for latency
to matter. LIBERO-90 fails the first criterion: neither π0.5
nor GR00T N1.7 was trained on it, and from its official
initial states they succeed in 28.3% and 16.8% of static
episodes (GR00T with its LIBERO-10 checkpoint). Within
the four standard suites, most tasks repeat one of the four
interactions or combine two subgoals. All ten LIBERO-
Object tasks put a grocery item into the basket (release), and
all ten LIBERO-Spatial tasks put the black bowl on the plate
(set-downwhen the plate moves,graspingwhen the bowl’s
support moves). Nine of the ten LIBERO-10 tasks combine
two subgoals, such as putting two items into the basket or
putting a bowl into a drawer and closing it. LIBERO-Goal’s
tasks set objects down, put the cream cheese into a bowl
(release), open a drawer or turn on the stove (articulated-
object manipulation), or open a drawer and then place the
bowl inside; the remaining task pushes the plate, so the
robot itself moves the target.
B EVALUATION AND MEASUREMENT
DETAILS
Targets follow fixed closed paths while the robot, the cam-
eras, the language instructions, the success checks, and
MuJoCo’s contact model stay as in LIBERO; this sec-
tion also defines the attempts, tolerance zones, and hand
speed behind Figures 7 and 11.Releaseuses LIBERO-
Object task 1, whose basket containment region is 12.2 cm
wide;set-downandgraspinguse LIBERO-Spatial task 9,
whose success check requires the bowl’s center within 3 cm
of the plate’s; andarticulated-object manipulationuses
LIBERO-Goal task 0. GR00T uses suite-specific check-
points (NVIDIA, 2026a), and MolmoAct2 uses one check-
point for all suites (Fang et al., 2026).
Each target moves at constant speed along one closed cu-
bic spline through 12 waypoints on an ellipse; the paths
span about 22×14 cm forrelease, 21×14 cm forset-down,
11×13 cm forgrasping, and 7×10 cm forarticulated-
object manipulation. We evaluate every task at 0.001 m/s
and from 0.025 to 0.2 m/s in 0.025 m/s steps. The grid stops
at 0.2 m/s, past the speed at which π0.5’s zero-latency suc-
cess falls to 50% on every task (0.04–0.19 m/s); at 0.2 m/s it
succeeds in 5.0–46.0% of episodes, against 93.0–98.5% near
static. We keep the background objects where LIBERO’s
original layouts place them, since perturbing initial object
states can sharply reduce policy success (Zhou et al., 2025);
inset-downthe stationary cabinet holding the bowl starts
at the same position in every layout, and ingraspingthe
bowl starts at the center of its moving support. Each condi-
tion’s 200 episodes cover LIBERO’s 50 official initial states,
each with two starting points on the path and both travel
directions.
Task deadlines.Each LIBERO episode has a budget of 600
executed policy actions, increased from 220–300 in standard
static LIBERO to allow more time to pursue moving targets.
An episode is considered a failure if the task remains in-
complete when this budget is exhausted. Stationary actions
inserted during inference waits do not consume this budget:
we extend the deadline by one control step for each such
idle step while the target continues moving. This preserves
the same executed-action budget across inference latencies
and execution modes.
A driver writes each target’s planar path at every physics
substep. The basket and the plate are free bodies whose
planar position and velocity are written while their height
and rotation stay physical; the bowl’s support moves on
added planar slide joints whose position and velocity follow
the path; and the drawer’s cabinet, a jointless LIBERO
fixture, is moved by position alone while the drawer keeps
its damped slide joint. No gravity, mass, friction, or solver
setting changes, so MuJoCo resolves every contact with its
unmodified contact model.
Following OpenPI’s protocol (Physical Intelligence, 2025),
each episode begins with 10 warm-up steps in which the
robot and the target stay still. In asynchronous modes, the
robot waits only for the first chunk and executes it from its
first action. Synchronous and lazy execution coincide at
zero latency; the policy comparison in Appendix C pools
their zero-latency runs where both exist, while the success
curves and Table 1 use synchronous runs. Intervals are 95%
Wilson confidence intervals.
A tolerance zone is the range of horizontal offsets from the
target center within which at least half of the attempts hit.
We pool attempts from all speeds, latencies, and modes,
retries included, into 0.5 cm bins of offset; a bin with at least
30 attempts belongs to the zone if at least half of them hit.
Hand speed is the hand’s path length per second while it
closes in on the target, over every qualifying interval after
warm-up, retries and inference waits included. Forrelease
andset-down, an interval qualifies while both fingers hold
the object within 15 cm horizontally of the receptacle or
surface and the hand is at or above the receptacle’s rim or the
surface; forgraspingandarticulated-object manipulation, it
qualifies while the empty hand is within 10 cm of the object
or handle without touching it. Figure 11 plots the median
episode hand speed at each target speed; the 34% reduction
in Section 4.2 compares the medians of these values over
target speeds.

D MODEL VALIDATION AND CALIBRATION
Fitted on 0, 100, and 200 ms runs, the model predicts the
held-out 50, 150, and 250 ms curves with an RMS error of
5.7 points for π0.5 and 7.8 points for MolmoAct2 (Table 4,
Figures 17 and 18). Sampling noise alone would make an
exactly right model miss these measurements by 4.0 points
RMS for π0.5 (3.9 for MolmoAct2), combining the held-out
points’ own binomial noise, 2.6 points (2.8), with that of the
measured reference curve each prediction reads.
Each prediction reads the reference curve, the measured
success curve of the reference configuration, at the target
speed scaled by λ from Equation 1. Between measured tar-
get speeds the curve interpolates the measured success rates
linearly, with no monotonicity imposed; below the slowest
measured speed it keeps that speed’s success rate; above
the fastest it follows a log-logistic curve fitted by maximum
likelihood to the reference’s own points and scaled to meet
the measured success at that speed. Apart from this tail,
which depends on the reference curve alone, the model’s
only free parameters are a and b, fitted by binomial maxi-
mum likelihood on the calibration conditions. For π0.5, the
model also predicts asynchronous execution at 50 ms with
intermediate horizons of H= 2 , 3, and 4, which no calibra-
tion includes, with RMS errors of 3.3 points onset-down
and 2.8 points onrelease.
Our model can be calibrated without zero-latency data from
one complete curve plus one point on each of two other
curves. Because SY (v)≈S X(v λY /λX) for any two set-
tings X and Y , the complete curve X, read as measured,
fixes the curve shape, and the two points constrain the ra-
tios λY /λX, from which a and b are fitted by maximizing
the binomial likelihood times a weak Gaussian prior over a
bounded grid. One of the three curves must be synchronous
at a nonzero latency, because only it separates idle time
from staleness.
We score every such calibration on the 0–250 ms grid for
π0.5 and MolmoAct2: each complete curve at 50 to 250 ms
in any mode, with one point in each of the other two ex-
ecution modes, both at the grid speed nearest that curve’s
midpoint speed divided by 1.4, where its measured success
first falls halfway between its highest and lowest value (Ta-
ble 5). On π0.5, the median RMS error over all remaining
points is 7.7 points, 80% of calibrations fall within 4.5–
14.4 points, and the unseen zero-latency curve is recovered
within a median of 6.8 points. MolmoAct2’s median is 11.1
points. GR00T N1.7, measured only at 0, 100, and 200 ms,
has a median of 11.6 points. Forπ0.5, measuring both points
on the sloped part of the curve, near the midpoint speed di-
vided by 1.4, matters: over every pair of grid speeds, its
median error rises to 9.4 points.
Table 6 lists the 99th-percentile latency of a single in-process
request at bf16 with openpi, run in PyTorch, and with
FlashRT (Su, 2026). For networked deployments, we add
approximately 5 ms of network latency over WiFi 7, 25 ms
over 5G, and 200 ms over a wired link to a slow cloud
region, following the estimates in VLA-Perf (Jiang et al.,
2026). System A’s latency is instead the median that VLA-
HWBench reports for π0.5 under FlashRT at 16-bit precision
on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor, with our workload of 10
actions and two camera images (Xu et al., 2026).

curve plus synchronous and continuous points at 200 ms,

We test the official DOM checkpoint of DynamicVLA (Xie
et al., 2026) in Isaac Sim onreleaseandgraspingat a 25 Hz
control rate with an execution horizon of H= 14 and a
chunk size of K= 20 . A driver writes the planar position
and velocity of each moving body before every control step:
the box inreleaseand, ingrasping, an unrendered rail carrier
that moves the can through contact alone (Figure 22). We
run synchronous execution at 0, 120, and 240 ms and lazy
and continuous execution at 120 and 240 ms, where 240 ms
(L= 6 ) is the longest latency satisfying K≥H+L . Each
setting runs 200 episodes at each target speed. Each episode

DOM’s native 300 to allow more time to pursue moving
targets, and DOM’s drop checks end it early as a failure if
the can or the box falls off the table.

task success on moving targets, to an extent that depends on
the task and target speed. Synchronous execution at 240 ms

F REAL-ROBOT EXPERIMENTS
We test the official checkpoint of MolmoAct2 for the
SO-100 and SO-101 arms (Fang et al., 2026) on an SO-101
arm (TheRobotStudio, 2025) with a front camera and a wrist
camera at a 30 Hz control rate with an execution horizon
of H= 16 and its default chunk size of K= 30 . We do
not test π0.5 or GR00T N1.7 on the robot because neither
has an official checkpoint for this arm, and, as in simulation
(§3.4), we evaluate only official public checkpoints rather
than policies we fine-tune ourselves.
We select four tasks that pair two interaction types with a
small and a big target: a 10×6 cm mini tote bag 7 cm tall
and a 20×7 cm case 8 cm tall as the objects to grasp, and a
bowl 15 cm in diameter and 5 cm tall and a plate 25 cm in
diameter and 2 cm tall as the receptacles. In(a) grasping
small, the robot picks up the moving tote bag and places it
in a stationary bowl. In(b) grasping big, the robot picks
up the moving case and places it on a stationary plate. In
(c) release smalland(d) release big, the robot picks up
the stationary tote bag and drops it into the moving bowl or
onto the moving plate (Figure 25).
Every moving target travels once, in one direction, along the
same straight 30 cm path, which ends as the target leaves
the camera’s view. A rollout succeeds if, by then, the tote
bag or case comes to rest in the bowl or on the plate and
stays there without tipping over. Forgrasping, the object
starts moving once the robot has executed its first action
chunk. Forrelease, the receptacle starts moving once the
robot has lifted the tote bag off the table. A motor pulls
the moving object through a transparent wire at a constant
0.02 or 0.05 m/s, and only one object moves at a time. Each
rollout starts with the arm and the objects reset to the same
positions.
We first check the setup with static targets, then move the
targets. Under synchronous execution, we place the target
of every task at the start, middle, and end of its path and test
every latency. Every static setting succeeds in all five roll-
outs, which confirms that the path, the object placement, and
the tasks themselves work before the targets move. We then
move the targets at 0.02 and 0.05 m/s under synchronous
execution on every task, and under lazy and continuous asyn-
chronous execution onrelease small, the harder release task,
to measure how much asynchronous execution recovers. We
set the latencies from MolmoAct2’s measured median in-
ference latency of 209 ms: holding each action chunk until
L control steps after its observation, we test 267, 367, and
467 ms (L= 8 , 11, and 14), all above the median so that
nearly every chunk takes effect at the set latency, with con-
tinuous execution at 267 ms (H=L= 8 ). Each reported
result is based on five rollouts.
Finding 1 holds.Inference latency substantially reduces
task success on moving targets, to an extent that depends on
the task and target speed. Unlike static targets, which never
fail, moving targets lose success: at 0.02 m/s, increasing
latency from 267 to 467 ms reducesgrasping smallsuccess
from three to zero of five rollouts andrelease smallsuccess
only from five to three (Figure 26(a,c)).
Finding 2 holds.A task’s latency sensitivity depends on its
requirements for action precision and continuous motion. At
0.02 m/s and 467 ms,grasping bigandrelease bigsucceed
in one and four of five rollouts, whereasgrasping small
andrelease small, which require more precision, succeed
in zero and three (Figure 26). A faster target requires faster
sustained motion: at 267 ms,release smallsucceeds in all
five rollouts at 0.02 m/s but in two at 0.05 m/s (Figure 26c).
Finding 3 holds.Asynchronous execution recovers task
success, and continuous asynchronous execution recovers
the most. Onrelease smallat 0.05 m/s and 267 ms, continu-
ous execution succeeds in all five rollouts, lazy execution in
four, and synchronous execution in two (Figure 27a).

G FAILURE STAGES
Latency costs success at the stages where the robot interacts
with the moving target, while the stages before and away
from it hold (Table 8). Each task is split into the stages
an episode passes on the way to success, and each rate
counts the episodes passing a stage among those that reached
it. Inrelease, the robot picks up the static object and lets
go of it over the moving receptacle; pickup means both
fingers hold the object and it rises at least 2 cm, and release
means both fingers then lose contact with it. Inset-down,
placement means the object rests on the moving surface
with stable contact for 0.2 s. Ingrasping, the robot picks up
the moving object and then places it on a static surface. In
articulated-object manipulation, the hand first touches the
handle of the moving drawer, and opening means the drawer
moves at least 1 cm from its starting position. Onrelease,
200 ms synchronous latency lowers success after release
from 80% to 58%, while pickup and release stay at or above
92%. Ongrasping, the loss is at pickup of the moving
object (85% to 73%); once the object is held, placing it
on the static surface succeeds about as often as without
latency. Asynchronous execution recovers these stages on
articulated-object manipulationand partly onrelease, but
little onset-downandgrasping.

H ADDITIONAL RELATED WORK
Several model and system papers handle inference latency
or execution speed within their own methods. On the sys-
tems side, SmolVLA introduces an asynchronous inference
stack that overlaps chunk prediction with execution and
compares it with synchronous execution on three real-world
tasks (Shukor et al., 2025), and VLASH conditions asyn-
chronous inference on robot states rolled forward with the
previous action chunk (Tang et al., 2026). On the policy-
pacing side, SAIL executes imitation policies faster than
their demonstrations and schedules actions around system
latency (Arachchige et al., 2025), DemoSpeedup acceler-
ates demonstrations by entropy-guided downsampling with-
out modeling inference delay (Guo et al., 2025), and Tem-
poVLA conditions π0.5 on a commanded speed (Jing et al.,
2026). Akinola et al. (2021) predict object motion for dy-
namic grasping because computation delays the robot’s re-
sponse.
