Does Validation Loss Predict Real-Robot Success?
Evidence from Four Imitation-Learning Policies
on a Low-Cost Arm
Simon Cowling
fysical.ai, Denmark
simon@fysical.ai
30 October 2026
Abstract
Held-out validation loss is the standard offline signal for choosing checkpoints and comparing
models, but its relation to closed-loop success on a real robot is rarely measured. We measure it for
ACT, Diffusion Policy, SmolVLA and π0.5 (action expert only), trained on nested subsets of 12 to
200 demonstrations of a pick-and-place task on an SO-101 arm. We score 82 checkpoints on 25
held-out demonstrations, with each policy’s training loss and with the error of the actions it outputs,
and compare the scores with 930 bench rollouts. We also score all 700 intermediate checkpoints of
70 training runs, and recover the ball positions of every training, held-out and bench episode from
the front camera. Within each policy, hold-out action error correlates with bench success (Spearman
ρ from −0.23 to −0.88), but no more strongly than the number of training episodes alone, and not
at all among seeds trained on the same data. Across policies the ranking is inverted ( ρ = +0.90 at
200 episodes): the policy with the lowest hold-out error succeeded in 3 of 110 rollouts. The most
common failure, the arm never leaving its rest pose, ended 96% of rollouts for Diffusion Policy trained
on untrimmed demonstrations and 47% for SmolVLA, and cannot occur in a hold-out set made of
demonstrations. Among rollouts in which the arm moved, SmolVLA’s hold-out action error predicts
success well (ρ = −0.81). For the diffusion and flow-matching policies, hold-out loss rises two- to
five-fold during training while hold-out action error falls by up to 29%. Held-out metrics are a sound
diagnostic of learning but no substitute for evaluation on the robot, and we recommend reporting
initiation failure and success among started rollouts as separate bench outcomes.
1 Introduction
Every rollout on a real robot costs minutes of attention and a reset by hand, so a number that could stand
in for the bench would save most of the cost of a study. Validation loss is the obvious candidate. It is
cheap, it is computed by the same code that trains the model, and in supervised learning it is the standard
way to choose a checkpoint and to compare models.
Robot imitation learning breaks two of the assumptions that make it work elsewhere. The policy is
trained on single steps but judged on a closed-loop run in which its own actions shape what it sees next,
so small errors compound [1]. And the demonstrations it is scored on always show the task already under
way, so some ways of failing never appear in them. Earlier work has found that validation loss is a weak
guide to success in this setting [2–4]; this study asks how weak, why, and what can replace it.
We answer these questions with the models and bench results of a previous scaling study [5], in which
ACT [6], Diffusion Policy [7], SmolVLA [8] and π0.5 [9] were trained on 12 to 200 demonstrations of
one task on one low-cost arm. The contributions are:
1. A held-out scoring method that works the same way for four very different policy families, and a
common measure, action error, that puts them on one scale.

2 Background
2.1 Validation loss
A policy learns to answer one question: given what the cameras and joint sensors show now, what did the
demonstrator do next? The training loss measures how wrong those answers are on the frames used for
training, which a large network can get right by memorising. Validation loss asks the same question on
demonstrations kept out of training. In this paper the 25 held-out demonstrations are called thehold-out,
and the same calculation on 12 demonstrations every model trained on is called thetraining probe.
2.2 What each policy’s loss measures
The four policies are trained on different objectives, so their losses are not on a common scale and cannot
be compared between models.
Only ACT’s loss is itself an error in the actions. For the other three, which are trained by denoising
diffusion [10] or flow matching [11], the loss scores how well the network repairs a deliberately corrupted
demonstration, averaged over every level of corruption. That is not the same as the quality of the action
the policy finally outputs.
2.3 Action error: one measure for every model
To compare models we also score the action each policy actually produces. The policy is run as it is
on the robot (its inference path and saved postprocessor), and its predicted chunk is compared with the
demonstrated actions over the steps the robot would execute before re-planning:

2.4 Related work
Offline evaluation of robot policies.The robomimic study found that the checkpoint with the lowest
validation loss could perform 50 to 100% worse on the robot than the best checkpoint [2]. A LeRobot
community experiment found Diffusion Policy’s validation loss rising while its simulated success im-
proved, and ACT’s staying flat while its success rose by more than 15% [3]. Restricting offline action
error to the task-critical segments of a demonstration improves its correlation with rollout success [ 4].
Other work replaces held-out loss with a different offline signal: off-policy classification predicts the
relative real-world performance of grasping policies trained in simulation [12], and matched simulated
environments have been used as a proxy for real-world evaluation of generalist manipulation policies [13].
The present study measures the gap between held-out metrics and real-robot success for four current
policy families on one task, and decomposes it by failure mode.
Why held-out error and closed-loop success diverge.A policy trained by behaviour cloning is
scored on states drawn from the demonstrations but deployed on states induced by its own actions, where
errors compound [1]. It can also rely on features that are correlated with the expert’s action without
causing it [14]. Policies given a history of observations can learn to repeat the expert’s previous action
instead of predicting the next one [15], and driving policies trained on data in which a stopped vehicle
usually stays stopped can fail to start moving again, the inertia problem [ 16]. The initiation failure
analysed in Section 5.4 has the same form. For diffusion policies trained on small datasets, the network
behaves much like a lookup table of training actions [17], which Section 6.1 uses to explain why their
hold-out loss and action error diverge.
Data scaling in imitation learning.Lin et al. found that the generalisation of imitation policies
to new environments and objects follows a rough power law in their number, and depends far more on
that diversity than on the absolute number of demonstrations [18]. The placement maps in Section 7 are
a small-scale analogue: what grows with the budget is how densely the training placements cover the
workspace.
3 Experimental setup
The models, demonstrations and bench protocol are those of [5], which reports the full training recipes
and bench results. This section summarises what the validation analysis needs.
3.1 Task and robot
An SO-101 arm [19] picks a green ball, placed among two red distractor balls on a 50 mm grid board, and
drops it in a basket. Each episode is recorded at 30 fps from a fixed low front camera and a wrist camera
(640×480), with the six joint positions and commanded actions.
3.2 Demonstrations, hold-out and budgets
The pool holds 225 teleoperated demonstrations, recorded in nine batches of 25 under slightly different
lighting. A frozen hold-out of 25 episodes was drawn stratified across the nine batches (data seed
20260922) and never used for training. From the other 200, nested training subsets were drawn once and
reused for every model and seed: S12 ⊂ S25 ⊂ S50 ⊂ S100 ⊂ S200. Because S12 sits inside every subset,
it doubles as the training probe. A second copy of the pool has the idle lead-in before the demonstrator’s
first movement removed (the trimmed data).

3.3 Models and training
All models were trained with LeRobot [20] at commit 8352ca62 (v0.6.2), using its default recipe for each
policy. We call one trained model, a combination of model line, budget and seed, a training cell.
ACT trains at a constant learning rate; Diffusion Policy decays its learning rate to zero at the last step
on a cosine schedule, and SmolVLA on a cosine schedule compressed to its run length. This matters when
reading the end of a training curve (Section 6).
3.4 Bench protocol
Each checkpoint was rolled out from a rest pose at ten standard ball placements, N00 to N09, in the
standard bench lighting of [5]: the room’s ordinary lighting of 117 to 394 lux, dimmer than the roughly
500 lux under which the demonstrations were recorded. Each rollout had a 30-second limit and was
labelled with one outcome: success, never started (the arm did not leave its rest pose), never located the
ball, grip failed, or another failure. As in [5], late starts and rollouts without a grasp attempt count as other
failures. The primary analysis set contains every rollout in this lighting at placements N00 to N09 from
the main grid and the repeat, control and weight-comparison sessions of [5]: 82 checkpoints and 930 of
the 1,340 rollouts reported there. Excluded are a probe in brighter light, an earlier π0.5 LoRA grid, and
two trimmed-data checkpoints trained with EMA but rolled out with plain weights; the last two have no
matching validation scores. We use the audited outcome labels of [ 5], in which 27 of the 1,340 labels
were corrected from the camera recordings.
4 Scoring method
4.1 How a checkpoint is scored
Each checkpoint is loaded with the normalisation saved alongside it and run in evaluation mode, without
gradients or image augmentation (ACT skips its V AE encoder; Diffusion Policy uses its centre crop).
Frames are batched one episode at a time, so every number also exists per episode and its uncertainty is a
standard error across episodes. For each checkpoint we record, on the hold-out and on the training probe:
• loss: the policy’s own objective, as in training; for the three generative policies averaged over four
draws of noise and time step with a fixed seed, so every checkpoint sees the same draws;
•action error: the mean absolute error of Section 2.3, overall and per joint.
Final checkpoints of ACT, Diffusion Policy and SmolVLA were scored on every frame (13,372 hold-
out frames, 6,139 probe frames). π0.5 was scored on every fifth frame in bfloat16, since one checkpoint
takes most of an hour. The 700 intermediate checkpoints were also scored on every fifth frame; on the
106 checkpoints scored both ways, this changed loss by a median of 0.8 to 3.5% and action error by 0.2 to
0.3%, and left the ranking of checkpoints unchanged (rank correlation 0.988 or higher). Plain and EMA

4.2 Statistics
• Rank correlation(Spearman’s ρ) between an offline measure and bench success across a model’s
checkpoints, with p-values from 20,000 permutations. Negative means lower error goes with higher
success.
• Expected from episodes alone.Success is simulated 5,000 times with each checkpoint’s success
probability set to its budget’s pooled rate, so the offline measure carries nothing beyond the budget;
the observedρis compared with the simulated range.
• Within-budget correlation.Both variables are centred on their budget means and ρ recomputed,
with 10,000 permutations within budgets. This asks whether the measure knows which seed did
better.
• Success ratesare pooled counts with 95% Wilson intervals; paired counts (EMA, trimming) use
Fisher’s exact test; seed differences use a chi-square summed over budgets with a simulated p-value.
4.3 Compute
Final checkpoints were scored on a single workstation GPU, taking about three minutes per ACT
checkpoint and under an hour per π0.5 checkpoint. Intermediate checkpoints were scored on NVIDIA
A10G GPUs through Hugging Face Jobs.
5 Final checkpoints and bench success
5.1 Both measures improve with data
For every model, bench success rises and hold-out action error falls from 12 to 200 episodes, with
diminishing returns (Figure 1). ACT goes from 1 success in 40 rollouts at 12 episodes to 37 of 40 at 100.
The pretrained models start higher: π0.5 succeeds in 16 of 40 rollouts with 12 episodes. The gap between
seen and unseen episodes narrows as data grows, and loss on the training probe rises, because a larger
training set is harder to fit exactly.
5.2 Within a model, validation adds nothing to the episode count
Across each model’s checkpoints, lower hold-out error goes with higher success. But the correlation is
no stronger than the episode count alone would produce, and among checkpoints trained on the same
episodes it vanishes.
Hold-out action error correlates with the logarithm of the episode count at−0.95 to−0.99 for every
model. It is close to a relabelling of how much data the model saw (Figure 2).
5.3 Across models, validation points the wrong way
At 200 episodes the ranking of the five model lines by hold-out action error and the ranking by success
correlate at +0.90: the lower the error, the worse the bench result. Untrimmed Diffusion Policy has
the lowest hold-out error in the study and succeeded in 3 of 110 rollouts. Part of this is the horizon
confound (ACT is scored over 100 steps, Diffusion Policy over 24), but the inversion survives within the
one like-for-like pair (Section 5.5).

5.4 Initiation failure is invisible to validation
The most common failure in the study is a policy that never moves the arm from its rest pose: 96% of
untrimmed Diffusion Policy rollouts, 47% of SmolVLA rollouts, 9% of trimmed Diffusion Policy rollouts,
and none for ACT or π0.5 (Figure 4). A hold-out made of demonstrations cannot contain this situation,
because the demonstrator always moved.
5.5 Once the arm starts, validation is informative
For SmolVLA, hold-out action error is only weakly related to how often the arm starts (ρ = −0.18) but
strongly related to success among rollouts that did start (ρ = −0.81). SmolVLA and π0.5 are the one pair
scored over the same 50-step horizon. At 200 episodes their hold-out errors are almost equal (4.98 and
5.11), and so are their success rates among started rollouts (71% and 73%). The difference in overall
success, 38% against 73%, is almost all initiation.
5.6 EMA and trimming change the bench, not the validation
We compare 11 pairs of models trained on the same data, budget and seed, one without and one with an
exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights; the two members of each pair were trained separately.
Their hold-out action error differs by at most 1.5% and their hold-out loss by at most 3.0%. On the bench,
π0.5 and trimmed Diffusion Policy are unchanged, but the two untrimmed Diffusion Policy models trained
with EMA started in 12 of 20 rollouts, against 2 of 20 for their counterparts trained without it (Fisher p =
0.002; Figure 6). The EMA models were retrained and benched on different days, so with two pairs the
difference cannot be attributed to EMA rather than to variation between training runs [5]. Either way, it is
a large difference in behaviour on the robot that validation did not register.
Trimming the idle lead-in from the training data took four paired Diffusion Policy cells from 2
successes in 40 rollouts to 34 in 70. Scored on their own validation data, the trimmed models looked
slightly worse (Figure 7). The trimmed and untrimmed hold-outs contain different frames, so the two sets
of numbers are not directly comparable, but nothing in either predicted success rising from 5% to 49%.

5.7 Seeds differ on the bench, not in validation
SmolVLA seeds trained on the same episodes differ on the bench by more than chance (simulated p =
0.04), mostly in how often the arm starts: at 200 episodes the four seeds started 2, 3, 6 and 10 of their ten
rollouts. Their hold-out action error differs by about 1%. ACT’s seed differences on the bench are within
chance (p = 0.11), and its hold-out error varies by 4 to 7% between seeds.
5.8 Loss on the training episodes orders the budgets backwards
A model trained on 12 episodes fits them almost perfectly, so its probe loss is the lowest in the study
while its bench success is the lowest. Probe loss therefore correlates positively with success: +0.86
for ACT, +0.67 for SmolVLA, +0.51 for π0.5. Training loss measures how closely a model fits its own
demonstrations, not how well it performs the task, and cannot be used to compare data budgets.
6 Training dynamics
We scored all 700 saved checkpoints of the 70 ACT, Diffusion Policy and SmolVLA runs, ten per run, on
the hold-out and on the training probe. Loss on the training probe falls to the last checkpoint for every
model and budget. This reflects fitting of the training set and does not show whether a model was trained
long enough; the hold-out curves do.
6.1 For the generative policies, loss and action error disagree
For ACT, hold-out loss and hold-out action error tell the same story: both drift down a few percent early
and then stay flat. For Diffusion Policy and SmolVLA they tell opposite stories (Figure 8). Hold-out
loss is lowest at the first saved checkpoint for all 15 of their model and budget combinations and climbs
steadily, to 3.5 to 5.1 times its starting value for untrimmed Diffusion Policy, 2.8 to 5.3 for trimmed and
2.0 to 2.9 for SmolVLA. Over the same steps, the error of the actions they produce stays level at small

The explanation lies in what the loss measures (Section 2.2). These models are trained to repair a
corrupted demonstration at every level of corruption. Trained long on a fixed set of demonstrations, the
network learns to pull any noisy input firmly towards the actions it has seen [17]. On a hold-out frame the
correct action is not one of those, so at many noise levels the repair is confidently wrong and the averaged
loss rises. The action the policy outputs comes from running the whole denoising chain, which converges
on a training-like action that fits the situation increasingly well. Action error scores that output.
Consequently, early stopping on hold-out loss would have halted every Diffusion Policy and SmolVLA
run at 10% of its schedule, where hold-out action error was up to 41% higher than at the end of training.
For ACT, whose loss is itself an action error, the two criteria agree.
6.2 Convergence on unseen demonstrations
On unseen demonstrations, only Diffusion Policy at 100 and 200 episodes was still improving when
training stopped.
• ACTreaches within 2% of its final hold-out action error by 20,000 to 40,000 steps at every budget.
Over the second half of training the change is −2.2% to +0.9%, inside the 4 to 7% spread between
seeds. ACT trains at a constant learning rate, so this plateau is the model’s own (Figure 9).
•Diffusion Policyat 12 to 50 episodes is within 2% of its final value by 10,000 to 30,000 steps. At
100 and 200 episodes (untrimmed) its hold-out action error fell 4.6% and 5.7% over the second half,
in all four seeds; a straight-line fit per seed gives p = 0.048 and 0.001. Trimmed Diffusion Policy
at 200 episodes points the same way (−2.7%, p = 0.10 with two seeds). No other combination of
model and budget shows a trend (p ≥ 0.10). Its cosine schedule drives the learning rate to zero at
the end, so the remaining gain may be larger than the curves suggest (Figure 10).

7 Spatial coverage of ball placements
7.1 Recovering positions from the front camera
We located all three balls at the start of every recorded episode and of one bench session, in millimetres on
the board. In each frame the 50 mm grid is detected and fitted, which gives the camera’s pose; each ball
is then fitted as a sphere of known size seen by that camera, so balls cut off by the frame edge or partly
hidden can still be placed.
The camera model is consistent across all 225 episodes: the front camera sits 82 mm above the board,
tilted 28◦ down, with a focal length of about 735 px. Fitted ball diameters come out at 29 mm (green) and
31 mm (red) with a spread under 1.5 mm, an independent check on the scale. Positions from frame 0 and
frame 30 of the same episode agree to 0.7 mm (median; 7 mm at most). Hiding parts of fully visible balls
on purpose shows that a ball with only 30% of its width in view is still placed to within 1.1 mm (median;
3.4 mm at the 90th percentile). In one episode the green ball was placed after recording began, and frame
30 was used. Relative to the grid, the camera shifted by about 5 mm between recording batches 1 to 4 and
5 to 9; positions are reported relative to the camera.
7.2 Coverage grows with the budget
Green start positions cover the whole floor the camera sees, about 200 mm across and 70 to 290 mm out
from the camera (Figure 13). How close a hold-out ball is to the nearest training ball shrinks steadily with
data: a median of 19 mm at 12 episodes, 12 mm at 50 and 5 mm at 200 (Figure 14). With 12 episodes, 9
of the 25 hold-out balls lie outside the area spanned by the training balls; from 50 episodes on, 5 do, all
at the edges of the camera’s view. This is the geometric side of Section 5.1: hold-out error falls as the
training set covers the hold-out’s situations more densely.

7.3 The bench placements
The ten bench placements were mapped from a single bench session (29 September 2026), chosen because
the ball positions logged for it were closest to the typical position of each placement across sessions.
The bench camera pose matches that of the recordings (82 mm high, tilted 27◦ down, rotated about 2.5◦
relative to them). Nine of the ten bench green balls lie inside the area spanned by the training balls at
every budget. N03, at the near edge of the view and 82 mm out, lies just outside it even at 200 episodes,
although a training ball is 6 mm away. At 200 episodes every bench green ball is within 11 mm of a
training ball (median 5 mm); at 12 episodes the median is 25 mm.
7.4 Distance from the training data and bench success
The two hardest placements are the two furthest from what the 12-episode models saw: N03 (22% success)
is 60 mm from the nearest S12 green ball and N00 (25%) 38 mm. The easiest, N02 (45%), is 9 mm away.
Across the ten placements, success and distance at S12 correlate atρ=−0.63 (p = 0.053).
Pooled over budgets, placements far from any training ball succeed less often ( ρ = −0.65 over 50
placement-by-budget cells), but distance and success both follow the budget. Within each budget the
relation is weaker and not significant (mean ρ = −0.26, permutation p = 0.08). It is clearest at 12 episodes
(ρ = −0.53) and absent at 200 (ρ = +0.15), once every placement has a training example within about 10
mm (Figure 16). With ten placements, distance is entangled with every other feature of a layout, such as
N03’s green ball being partly out of view, so the association is suggestive rather than established.

improving on unseen data early in its schedule; and models trained with and without EMA score almost
identically. The placement maps show the mechanism: the situations in the hold-out are covered more

What it does not measure is whether the policy, run closed loop from a rest pose, will do the task.
The study shows that gap in four independent ways: the ranking across models is inverted, seeds with
indistinguishable validation numbers differ on the bench, EMA and trimming changed the bench without
moving validation, and for generative policies the loss itself moves the wrong way during training.
8.2 Why validation cannot see initiation failure
The failure that separates SmolVLA and Diffusion Policy from ACT and π0.5, and SmolVLA’s seeds from
one another, is the arm never starting. A hold-out drawn from demonstrations cannot contain it, because
the demonstrator always moved. The frames closest to it are the idle lead-in at the start of an untrimmed
demonstration, where the correct answer is to hold still, which is exactly what a policy that never starts
does. An idle-prone policy is rewarded by those frames and penalised by none. The mechanism resembles
the inertia problem described for driving policies [16]: where the data show a stationary agent usually
remaining stationary, being at rest becomes a predictor of staying at rest.
This is why trimming is the most informative single experiment. Removing the idle lead-in removed
the behaviour from training and the frames that rewarded it from validation, so the trimmed models’
validation numbers got slightly worse while their bench success rose from 5% to 49%. A validation set
that could detect initiation failure would need rest-pose observations paired with the demonstrator’s first
movement as the target, which is the opposite of what a recording contains.
Lighting adds to the blind spot. The bench ran in the room’s ordinary lighting, dimmer than the
recordings. In the brighter-light probe of [ 5], untrimmed Diffusion Policy started in 16 of 50 rollouts,
against 2 of 50 for the same checkpoints in dim light, and SmolVLA in 36 of 50 against 32 of 50. Part of
the initiation failure is therefore a response to a change in conditions between recording and deployment,
which a hold-out recorded under the training conditions cannot reveal either.

8.3 Loss and action error for generative policies
For diffusion and flow-matching policies, the loss scores the repair of a corrupted demonstration at every
noise level, not the action the policy outputs. As these models memorise their training actions [17], the
repair of unseen actions gets worse while the output improves. Their hold-out loss is therefore not a
measure of generalisation in the usual sense, and it inverts the usual reading of a validation curve.
Action error has limits of its own. Errors grow with the horizon, so levels differ by policy for
mechanical reasons. And the sampling policies choose one of several plausible motions: on a frame where
the demonstrator could reasonably have done two things, they are sometimes scored as wrong for doing
the other. ACT, which regresses towards the average motion, is not penalised in that way, which may be
part of why its error is the highest in the study while its success is the highest of the models trained from
scratch.
8.4 Why seeds do not separate
A success rate from ten rollouts carries about ±15 percentage points of uncertainty; hold-out error is
averaged over 13,372 frames and is precise to a few percent. Among seeds that all start, the bench
differences are mostly rollout noise. Among seeds that do not all start, the difference is initiation, which
validation does not see. In both cases there is nothing for a within-budget correlation to find.
8.5 Implications for evaluation practice
These results suggest a division of labour between offline metrics and evaluation on the robot. Hold-out
action error is a sound criterion for deciding when to stop training: it plateaued within 20 to 40% of
ACT’s schedule, and it identified Diffusion Policy at 100 and 200 episodes as the only configurations
still improving when training ended. For diffusion and flow-matching policies, hold-out loss should not
be used for this purpose, because it rises from the first checkpoint while the policy’s outputs improve.
Whenever action error is reported, its horizon should be stated. Validation is also useful for detecting
broken runs, incorrect episode lists and faulty training recipes, which appear as outliers against the rest of
a grid.
On this evidence, hold-out metrics should not be used to choose between seeds, between converged
checkpoints or between model families, or to decide which configurations merit robot evaluation: in this
study the configuration with the lowest hold-out error performed worst on the bench. Training loss should
not be used to compare data budgets, since it orders them backwards.
For bench evaluation, reporting initiation failure separately from other failure modes, and success
among the rollouts in which the policy started alongside overall success, divides closed-loop performance
into a component that held-out imitation metrics track and one they cannot observe. An offline test for
initiation would have to be constructed deliberately (Section 10).
9 Limitations
All results come from one pick-and-place task on one SO-101 setup in one laboratory, and should be read
as a detailed case study rather than a general law. The bench lighting was dimmer than the lighting under
which the demonstrations were recorded, so bench success reflects robustness to that change as well as
generalisation to new placements. Most checkpoints have ten bench rollouts, about ±15 percentage points
of uncertainty each; the analysis therefore relies on pooled rates and paired comparisons, and differences
between single training cells should not be over-read. The grids are uneven: π0.5 has two seeds (seed
3 only with EMA weights) and saved only its halfway and final checkpoints, the untrimmed Diffusion
Policy line was benched with two seeds per budget, and the trimmed line has two seeds.
Action error is scored over each policy’s own execution horizon (24 to 100 steps), so SmolVLA against
π0.5 is the only like-for-like comparison of levels. π0.5 and all intermediate checkpoints were scored on
every fifth frame, and π0.5 in bfloat16; the effect of the stride was checked (Section 4.1), the effect of

reduced precision was not. Trimmed and untrimmed models are validated on different hold-out frames,
so their validation numbers are not directly comparable. The end of the Diffusion Policy and SmolVLA
curves is shaped by their learning-rate schedules, taken to be LeRobot’s defaults, so the remaining gain
for Diffusion Policy at 100 and 200 episodes is uncertain.
Bench results exist only for final checkpoints, so the assumption that bench success follows hold-out
action error along a training run is not tested. Bench ball positions were mapped from a single session, and
placements shift by a few millimetres between sessions; ten placements are too few to separate distance
from the training data from other features of a layout.
10 Future work
Several questions raised by these results can be addressed offline, with the existing recordings, checkpoints
and bench record. The most direct is an offline initiation probe: presenting each policy with the opening
frames of the hold-out demonstrations, while the arm is still at rest, and measuring whether its predicted
actions would move the arm within the first second. If this quantity tracks each checkpoint’s bench rate
of starting, it would provide an offline test for the failure mode that validation cannot observe. Two
refinements of action error are also open: restricting it to the approach and grasp segments, following [4],
and scoring all policies over a common 24-step horizon to remove the horizon confound from cross-model
comparisons. Finally, Diffusion Policy at 100 and 200 demonstrations was still improving on unseen data
when training stopped, and its behaviour under a longer schedule remains to be measured.
11 Conclusion
Validation loss and hold-out action error are good measures of learning and poor measures of performance
on the robot. Across four policy families, five data budgets and 930 bench rollouts, they show cleanly that
every model learns more from more demonstrations, that pretrained models generalise from fewer, that
ACT and SmolVLA stopped improving on unseen data well before their schedules ended, and that only
Diffusion Policy with 100 or more demonstrations was still improving.
They predict bench success no better than the episode count does, say nothing about which seed will do
better, and rank models in the wrong order. The reason is a specific blind spot rather than noise: the most
common failure in the study, the arm never starting, cannot appear in a hold-out made of demonstrations.
Where that failure is absent, among rollouts that started, hold-out error and success agree well. For
diffusion and flow-matching policies the hold-out loss is misleading even as a training diagnostic, rising
from the first checkpoint while the actions they produce improve.
Hold-out action error is therefore suited to deciding when to stop training and to detecting broken runs.
Choosing between seeds, checkpoints and model families still requires evaluation on the robot, which
is more informative when success among the rollouts in which the policy started is reported alongside
overall success.
