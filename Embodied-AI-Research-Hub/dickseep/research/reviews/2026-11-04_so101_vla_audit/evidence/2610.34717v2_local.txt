ABSTRACT
Vision–language–action (VLA) models increasingly run on edge devices aboard
robots, where inference latency constrains closed-loop robot control. However, it
remains unclear how to design VLA models, their inference software and edge
hardware so that robots can run VLA inference in real time. Most VLA models
report only task success, and prior studies cover few of the ways recent VLAs
generate actions, split a call into at most three phases and do not compare inference
optimizations on the same models. Therefore, we introduceVLA-HWBench, a
benchmark that measures 18 open VLA models on their released software and on
alternative backends, on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor and an AMD Ryzen AI
Max+ PRO 395, with a seven-stage breakdown of each call. Our study reveals
three insights. First, where the time goes depends on how a model generates
actions, not on its size. Action generation is the largest stage in 14 of the 19
configurations on the Jetson, latency spans 82× on the Jetson and 157× on the
AMD platform, and parameter count explains at most 15% of its variance. Models
that generate long action chunks in a few flow-matching steps keep up with control,
whereas token-by-token decoding does not. Second, kernel fusion with 4-bit
weights helps most, running π0.5 and GR00T N1.7 2.6–2.9× faster than PyTorch
with torch.compile, but it exists for only these two models and only on the
Jetson. Optimization also moves the time to the stages it shortens least, as CPU
preprocessing grows from 12% to 39% of the GR00T N1.7 call. Third, after
optimization, memory bandwidth limits only the stages that process few tokens on
the Jetson, such as the denoising steps, whereas software limits all other stages,
from unfused kernels to serial preprocessing on the host CPU. Overall, low-latency
edge VLAs should use few action-generation iterations, inference software should
extend kernel fusion and low-bit weights to more models and platforms, and edge
hardware needs high memory bandwidth and a fast host CPU.
1 INTRODUCTION
Vision–language–action (VLA) models have emerged as a promising approach to general-purpose
robotic control, mapping visual observations and language instructions directly to robot actions (Bro-
han et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Octo Model Team et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025b; Bjorck et al.,
2025). Unlike text or image generation, VLA inference operates within a continuously evolving
physical system: the robot may continue executing previous commands while objects or obstacles
change state or position (Falanga et al., 2019; Morrison et al., 2018), causing observations to become
stale before the corresponding actions are executed. Practical deployment therefore increasingly calls
for low-latency inference close to the robot. This is particularly important when wireless connectivity
is variable or intermittent, or when privacy-sensitive camera observations cannot be transmitted to
remote servers. Meanwhile, advances in edge compute hardware (NVIDIA, 2025; AMD, 2025) are
making on-robot VLA inference increasingly feasible.
However, the observation-to-action characteristics of state-of-the-art VLA models on edge devices
have not been systematically characterized. Recent studies have begun to characterize VLA inference
through measurements on edge and datacenter GPUs (Taherin et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026), profiling

To bridge these gaps and provide a systematic basis for practical on-robot deployment, we present
VLA-HWBench, a benchmark for characterizing the complete observation-to-action path of state-of-
the-art VLA models on edge devices.VLA-HWBenchis designed to incorporate additional VLA
models, inference backends, and edge hardware as the ecosystem evolves.
First, to cover the forms of action generation,VLA-HWBenchevaluates18widely used open VLA
models spanning all four forms, selected based on Hugging Face downloads and community stars, on
two architecturally distinct edge platforms, the NVIDIA Jetson AGX Thor and AMD Ryzen AI Max+
PRO 395. Each model runs in a common reference configuration using eager PyTorch, with optional
optimizations disabled, on the same LIBERO-10 observation (Liu et al., 2023) and timing boundary.
Second, to show where the time goes,VLA-HWBenchdivides each call into the seven stages of
Figure 2, timed at the stage boundaries and split by kernels inside CUDA graphs. Third, to study
optimizations systematically,VLA-HWBenchenables compilation, specialized backends (TensorRT
and FlashRT) and lower precision one at a time on two representative models and attributes each gain
to stages. WithVLA-HWBench, we answer three questions.
(Q1) Where does the time go in VLA models, and what sets their latency?Without optional
optimizations, action generation is the largest stage in 14 of the 19 configurations on the Jetson,
and latency ranges from 77 ms to 6.3 s on the Jetson (82×) and from 114 ms to 17.9 s on the AMD
platform (157×), with only 10 and 9 of the 19 configurations generating actions as fast as LIBERO
executes them. As Figure 1 shows, a least-squares fit of log latency on log parameter count explains at
most 15% of the variance, and latency instead follows the number of iterations of action generation.
(Q2) How much do optimizations help, and where does the time move?On the Jetson, compilation,
a specialized backend and lower precision make π0.5 2.5×, 1.8× and 1.6× faster in turn, 7.2× in
total, and GR00T N1.7 1.02×, 1.4× and 1.8×, 2.6× in total. A backend with kernel fusion and
4-bit weights gives the largest gain, 2.6–2.9× over compilation, but exists only for these two models
and only on the Jetson. Optimization also moves the time to the stages it shortens least, as CPU
preprocessing grows from 12% to 39% of the GR00T N1.7 call.

(Q3) What limits the remaining time after optimization?No single resource limits the remaining
time. Action generation and other stages with few tokens are memory-bound and on the Jetson
already run at more than half of the peak memory bandwidth, whereas vision encoding and long
prefills reach only 6–42% of what the hardware allows because of unfused kernels and missing
low-precision support, and CPU preprocessing is limited by serial Python and idle-thread wake-ups.
Overall, the latency of a VLA on edge hardware is determined jointly by its action-generation method,
its runtime backend and the platform rather than by its model size. For future designs, edge VLAs
should therefore reduce action-generation iterations, for example through fewer denoising steps or
parallel decoding, rather than simply shrink the model; inference software should extend kernel
fusion and low-bit execution, which provide the largest gains, to more models and platforms while
optimizing host preprocessing as carefully as GPU execution; and edge hardware should prioritize
memory bandwidth and a fast host CPU over peak GPU compute.

VLA models and action generation.Released VLA models (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024;
Octo Model Team et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025b; Bjorck et al., 2025) generate an action chunk

four datacentre GPUs, Zhou et al. (2026) profile seven policies, five of them VLAs, on accelerators

3.2 METRICS
To systematically answer the three research questions, we define a corresponding set of metrics, each
targeting the key property examined by one question.
Q1, where the time goes.The first question compares the stage shares Tk/T , the latency T and the
inference time per action T /H across models in their reference configurations, which run each model
without optional optimizations. LIBERO’s simulator runs its controller at 20 Hz (Liu et al., 2023;
Zhu et al., 2020), so it executes one action every 50 ms, and a model keeps up only if T /H <50 ms.
Q2, optimizations.The second question measures the speed-up T0/T of an optimization, where T0
is the latency of the reference configuration and T the latency with the optimization. It also compares
the actions before and after each compilation and precision step.
Q3, bottlenecks.The third question asks what limits the fastest configurations, using the roofline
model (Williams et al., 2009). A GPU stage with F floating-point operations and W bytes of weights
takes at least its compute bound F/P or its memory bound W/B, whichever is larger, where P
is the peak matrix-unit throughput in its number format and B the peak memory bandwidth. It is
memory-bound if F/W lies below the ridge point P/B . A stage close to its bound is limited by the
hardware, and one far from it by its software.
3.3 DESIGNPRINCIPLES
Consistent runtime backends.We run each model through the inference entry point of its own
LIBERO evaluation with the library versions its authors pin, compare models only in their reference
configurations, and add optimizations one at a time on fixed weights and input.
Stage-level attribution.We synchronize the device at every stage boundary of each release’s own
code, check that the stages add up to the end-to-end latency, and separate the stages that a runtime
captures in one CUDA graph by the graph’s kernels.
Two edge platforms.Two platforms with Arm and x86 hosts, which differ by 4.4× in peak 16-bit
throughput but only1.1×in memory bandwidth, separate the effects of host, GPU and memory.

4VLA-HWBENCHBENCHMARKDESIGN
4.1 MODELCOVERAGE
We select 18 open VLA models that were released in 2025 or 2026, report strong LIBERO success,
publish a LIBERO checkpoint with its evaluation code and are widely used, and that together cover the
four main forms of action generation. Table 2 lists them, and Appendix Table 5 gives the GitHub stars
of their code and the downloads of their checkpoints. Each model runs its LIBERO-10 checkpoint
with the settings of its own LIBERO evaluation, which Appendix Table 4 details, and MolmoAct2’s
two action heads give 19 reference configurations.
4.2 RUNTIMEBACKENDS
The models run on four runtime backends.
• LeRobot 0.6.1(Cadene et al., 2024; 2026) is a library for robot learning that runs each policy as a
PyTorch program and offerstorch.compileas an option.
• The authors’ codeis the inference entry point of a release’s own LIBERO evaluation, which runs
the model as a PyTorch program with the library versions the authors pin.
• FlashRT(Su, 2026) is an inference runtime for VLA models that replaces the PyTorch program
with fused kernels in captured CUDA graphs and offers FP8 and NVFP4 weights.
•TensorRT(NVIDIA, 2026) compiles each part of a model into an optimized inference engine.
Q1 runs every model in its reference configuration, on LeRobot or the authors’ code with every
optional optimization off. Q2 adds the optimizations of all four backends one at a time to the reference
configurations of π0.5 and GR00T N1.7, and Q3 analyzes the fastest configurations of Q2. FlashRT
and TensorRT run only on the Jetson, and Appendix F reports the options of the other models.

4.4 MEASUREMENTPROTOCOL
Input and timing.Every configuration processes the same real LIBERO-10 observation (Liu et al.,
2023), two 256×256 camera images, the robot state and the task instruction, adapted only where
a release’s input contract requires it. Each configuration runs in its own process with 20 warm-up
calls and up to 100 timed calls, trimmed to fit 90 s but never below 20, each bracketed by device
synchronization, and we report the median latency. To check how the content of the input affects
latency, we also run every configuration on 24 different frames of one episode.
Stage attribution.Synchronized timers at the stage boundaries of each model’s own software
measure the stages of Equation 1, which Appendix Table 6 details. The reference configurations run
without CUDA graphs, so each boundary that a release’s code exposes is timed directly, including the
vision-encoder and prefill calls inside LeRobot’s policies, and a stage that the code runs inside another
one is counted in that one. We get the costbof one denoising step or camera view by fitting latency
as a+bN over their number N, and we split the CUDA graphs of the optimized configurations into
stages by the order of their kernels and how often each repeats, once per step or layer.
Profiling.The profiler in Pytorch traces the GPU kernels of the optimized configurations, which we
sort into GEMM or attention kernels on the matrix units, GEMMs without them and other kernels,
and records the shapes and number formats of every operator in an eager run of the same workload,
which give F and W of each stage. For CPU preprocessing, we time its sub-steps, vary the number
of CPU threads and read the cores’ idle-state counters.

Where the time goes.Figure 3 shows that action generation is the largest stage in 14 of the 19
configurations on the Jetson, with 42–99% of the call, as in prior work (up to 75%, Vishwanathan
et al., 2026). The five exceptions, the four parallel decoders and Evo-1, spend more of the call in
prefill or vision encoding than in action generation, as does π0.5 on the AMD platform, whose FP32
vision tower encodes three image slots, one empty.
Latency range.Figure 4a shows that latency ranges from 77 ms for X-VLA to 6.3 s for VLA-0 on
the Jetson (82×) and from 114 ms to 17.9 s on the AMD platform (157×), with a similar ranking
(Spearman’sρ= 0.91 ). Releases generate 5 to 50 actions per call, and Figure 4b shows that long
chunks hide slow calls, as π0.5 spends less time per action than VLA-Adapter though its call takes
4.4× longer. Only 10 of the 19 configurations on the Jetson and 9 on the AMD platform keep up
with LIBERO’s control rate.
Model size.In Figure 1, a least-squares fit of log latency on log parameter count, one point per
checkpoint, explains only 9% of the variance on the Jetson and 15% on the AMD platform. GR00T
N1.7 and VLA-0 hold 3.1 and 3.8 B parameters, yet on the Jetson VLA-0 is 53× slower, and
OpenVLA-OFT holds 5.2× the parameters of VLA-Adapter, also a parallel decoder, but is only2.7×
slower per call.
Iterations.Equation 2 accounts for the gap. On the Jetson, one flow-matching step costs 4–107 ms
and parallel decoding needs a single iteration, whereas autoregressive heads pay one language-model
pass of 20–52 ms per token, so the 208 decimal tokens of VLA-0 take 99% of its call and the reasoning
tokens of DeepThinkVLA and MolmoAct2-Think about 91%. On identical weights, MolmoAct2’s
discrete head is 2.3× slower than its flow head, and the four configurations with scene-dependent
token counts vary by 12–122% across 24 frames.
Insight 1.In most of the 19 reference configurations,the time goes mainly to action generation,
and latency follows the number of its iterations rather than the parameter count, so token-
by-token decoding and reasoning cannot keep up with LIBERO’s 20 Hz control whereas flow
matching over long action chunks can.Future edge VLAs should cut these iterations, with
fewer denoising steps, parallel decoding or longer action chunks, rather than shrink the model.

whereas TensorRT gains less for GR00T N1.7, as it keeps the vision encoder in FP32. A backend
also changes the number format, since FlashRT runsπ 0.5’s FP32 layers in FP16.
Lower precision.FP8 and NVFP4 make both models another 1.3–1.8 × faster, mostly in action
generation. FP8 keeps the cosine similarity to the FP16 actions at 0.998 or higher and NVFP4 at 0.93
or higher. In total,π 0.5 falls from 318 to 44 ms on the Jetson and GR00T N1.7 from 119 to 46 ms.
Where the time moves.Figure 5c,d shows that the stages shrink unevenly, so the time moves between
them. Vision encoding falls from 33% to 13% of the π0.5 call and prefill rises from 14% to 34%, and
GR00T N1.7’s CPU preprocessing, which no optimization shortens, grows from 12% to 39%.
On the AMD platform.Only compilation exists there, so optimization widens the platform gap
from 2.0–2.5×to 5.7–7.8×.
Insight 2.Optimization makes π0.5 7.2× and GR00T N1.7 2.6× faster on the Jetson, withkernel
fusion and 4-bit weights giving the largest gain, andmoves the remaining time to the stages
it shortens least, such as CPU preprocessing. But fused 4-bit backends exist for only two of the
18 models and only on the Jetson, soinference software should bring kernel fusion and low-bit
execution to more models and vendors, and model code should compile as one graph.
5.3 WHATLIMITS THEREMAININGTIMEAFTEROPTIMIZATION?
We analyze the fastest configurations of Q2, FlashRT with NVFP4 on the Jetson and compilation on
the AMD platform, whose stages Figure 6a shows. Figure 6c,d place the GPU stages on rooflines,
and Figure 6b and Appendix Figure 7 split them further.
Action generation is memory-bound.A denoising step processes only the 10–41 tokens of the
action chunk and robot state, so on the Jetson it performs only 20–150 operations per byte of weights,
far below the FP8 and NVFP4 ridge points of 1,900 and 3,800, and is bounded by reading its weights.
FlashRT’s steps take only 1.6–1.8× this bound, as their GEMMs read the weights at three quarters
of the peak bandwidth and short normalization, attention and quantization kernels add a third or
more to the GEMM time. Action generation on the Jetson is thus limited by the memory bandwidth,
a hardware limit that the few action tokens set. On the AMD platform, the steps stay 2.8–3.3 ×
below the roof, as the compiled GEMMs use less of the bandwidth and Isaac-GR00T recomputes the
context’s keys and values in every step, which FlashRT does once per call.

Prefill length decides its bound.The 574–712 prefix tokens of π0.5 put its prefill near or above the
ridge. On the AMD platform it is compute-bound, but it reaches only 42% of the BF16 peak, and a
fifth of its tokens are LeRobot’s prompt padding. On the Jetson, it lies left of the ridge but stays3.7×
below the roof, as kernels besides the weight GEMMs, such as unfused attention and re-quantization,
take half of its time. GR00T N1.7 prefills only 156 tokens and is memory-bound, taking 1.7× its FP8
weight-read time on the Jetson, whereas on the AMD platform its GEMMs read the weights at a fifth
of the peak bandwidth. A long prefill is thus limited by its kernels, and a short one by the memory
bandwidth on the Jetson and by its GEMMs on the AMD platform.
Vision encoding misses the matrix units on the AMD platform.The vision tower encodes hundreds
of patch tokens per image and is compute-bound on the AMD platform, where it is one of the two
largest stages of both models, yet runs largely without the matrix units. LeRobot runs π0.5’s tower in
FP32, for which RDNA 3.5 has no matrix instructions, and ROCm’s MIOpen executes GR00T N1.7’s
3D patch embedding as 512 matrix-vector products without them, so the towers stay 8× and 17×
below the roof. On the Jetson, FlashRT’s FP8 towers are memory-bound but 2.9–3.7× below the
roof, as normalization, quantization and attention take a third to half of their time. Vision encoding is
thus limited by software, by the support for its number format and operators on the AMD platform
and by its kernels on the Jetson.
CPU preprocessing is limited by host software.CPU preprocessing is the largest stage of GR00T
N1.7 on the Jetson, 39% of the fastest call, as no optimization touches Isaac-GR00T’s input processor
and data collator, which prepare images and prompt in Python and PyTorch. Figure 6b shows that a
third of it goes to waking PyTorch’s 14 worker threads from the deepest idle state their cores enter
while the GPU runs, for each small parallel operation, a cost that one thread avoids, and the rest is
serial work on one Arm core that the AMD host finishes 5× faster. It is thus limited by host software
and its thread configuration, not by the GPU.
Insight 3.After optimization,no single resource limits VLA inference, as memory bandwidth
limits the few-token stages such as the denoising steps on the Jetson, kernels limit vision encoding
and prefill, and the host CPU limits preprocessing.Edge hardware thus needs bandwidth and a
fast CPU more than peak compute, andinference software must fuse the kernels around the
GEMMs and streamline preprocessing, which bandwidth alone cannot fix.

6 CONCLUSION
We introducedVLA-HWBench, which measures where the inference time of released VLA models
goes on edge devices, stage by stage. Across 18 open models on two edge platforms, latency differs
by up to 157× and follows the number of iterations of action generation rather than model size; a
backend with kernel fusion and 4-bit weights gives the largest gain, 2.6–2.9 × over PyTorch with
torch.compile, but exists only on the Jetson and for two models, and optimization moves time
to the stages it shortens least; and after optimization memory bandwidth limits only the stages that
process few tokens on the Jetson, mainly the denoising steps, while software limits the rest. These
insights can guide the design of edge VLA models, inference software and hardware.

G BOTTLENECKANALYSIS
Figure 7 splits the time of every GPU stage of Figure 6c,d into kernel classes, and Table 15 lists the
numbers behind these panels. The floating-point operations F and weight bytes W of each stage
come from the GEMM shapes of the eager run of the same model and workload and are counted
in the number format of each configuration’s kernels. FlashRT runs the vision tower and the action
expert of π0.5 with FP8 weights and only the FFN of its language model with NVFP4 weights, runs
the vision tower and the language model of GR00T N1.7 with FP8 weights and every GEMM of
its action head with NVFP4 weights, and computes the adaptive-norm modulation of the denoising
steps and the keys and values of the GR00T N1.7 context once per call, so that a FlashRT step reads
only the weights of its GEMMs. The compiled configurations keep the formats of the loaded models,
LeRobot pads the π0.5 prompt to 200 tokens, and Isaac-GR00T recomputes the modulation and the
keys and values of the context in every step and computes the output projection of its language model
over the whole vocabulary in prefill. The time of a stage inside a captured graph is the kernel time of
its part of the trace, or for GR00T N1.7 on FlashRT that part’s share of the timed graph, and the time
of a GR00T N1.7 step is the slope of its step sweep. The ratio divides the time by the larger of the
two bounds, at the peaks of Table 3.
